# Gastric Cancer Surgery Checklist: Nutritional Prep & Surgical Clearances

Stomach cancer can affect eating well before an operation is discussed. Early fullness, reduced appetite, vomiting, bleeding or weight loss may leave a patient weaker at the very time major treatment is being planned. A useful preparation visit therefore considers nutrition and function alongside the tumour's site and stage. It should also establish whether surgery is the first treatment or one part of a combined approach.

This gastric cancer surgery checklist explains the discussions before partial or total gastrectomy, including staging, dietetic support, medicines, anaesthesia and recovery arrangements. It distinguishes removing part of the stomach from removing all of it and explains why the way you eat and obtain nutrients may change afterwards.

For Indian patients and families, practical planning includes obtaining original endoscopy and biopsy records, coordinating oncology appointments and arranging support with meals after discharge. Ask for advice that works with your usual foods and budget. Do not begin a restrictive diet or delay recommended cancer treatment while trying an unproven supplement programme. The plan should be agreed by your treating specialists.

## Confirm the cancer diagnosis and treatment sequence

The biopsy report identifies the type of disease; imaging and other investigations help assess its extent. A cancer at the junction of the food pipe and stomach may need a different discussion from one lower in the stomach. Ask the team to show where the tumour is and explain the current stage in plain language.

Treatment may include surgery, systemic therapy or other approaches depending on stage and health. Selected very early lesions may have an endoscopic option, while more extensive disease can require a different strategy. The [NCI stomach-cancer treatment guide](https://www.cancer.gov/types/stomach/treatment) describes these distinctions without making one operation appropriate for everyone.

Ask whether a multidisciplinary team has reviewed the case and how the sequence was chosen. If chemotherapy is planned before surgery, clarify who will reassess fitness and disease extent afterwards. Do not interpret treatment before an operation as proof that surgery has been abandoned.

### What does gastrectomy involve?

A subtotal or partial gastrectomy removes part of the stomach. Total gastrectomy removes the whole stomach and requires a new connection so swallowed food can continue into the intestine. Lymph nodes are assessed or removed as part of the cancer operation according to the plan.

The exact extent depends on the tumour, margins and anatomy. Ask whether a laparoscopic, robotic or open approach is proposed and what could change during the operation. A smaller incision does not mean the cancer treatment is automatically less extensive or the nutritional consequences disappear.

## Staging and fitness answer different questions

Staging investigates where the cancer is. Fitness assessment considers how you are likely to tolerate treatment. Both may need review after chemotherapy or a significant change in health. Bring the actual scan images and pathology access details so another centre can review them without relying only on a short summary.

| Investigation or assessment | What it helps clarify | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and biopsy | Tumour location and tissue diagnosis | In establishing the diagnosis |
| CT staging | Extent of disease and operative planning | According to the oncology pathway |
| Additional imaging or staging laparoscopy | Resolves a particular question about spread | When the result could alter treatment |
| Blood count and relevant deficiency tests | Anaemia or nutritional concerns | According to symptoms and results |
| Kidney, liver and electrolyte tests | Treatment and anaesthesia planning | As directed by the clinical team |
| Dietetic and functional assessment | Intake, weight change and ability to recover | Particularly with weight loss or reduced activity |

Ask which results are already adequate and which need updating. Buying repeated “clearance” packages does not replace review of the findings. Tell the team if you have black stools, vomiting blood, worsening vomiting or difficulty maintaining fluids; significant bleeding or obstruction needs urgent assessment.

## Make nutritional preparation practical

Record recent weight change and what you can manage in a normal day. Describe early fullness, swallowing difficulty, nausea, vomiting and any foods you now avoid. These details help a dietitian identify whether the problem is inadequate intake, obstruction, treatment side effects or another issue requiring action.

The [NICE oesophago-gastric cancer guideline](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng83/chapter/recommendations) recommends tailored dietetic support around radical treatment. This is not a request to force large meals. Smaller portions, prescribed supplements or another form of support may be discussed according to the situation, especially when the usual route is not adequate.

Ask how to include familiar foods, protein sources and affordable alternatives. Vegetarian preferences, diabetes, kidney problems and food intolerance should be considered together rather than managed through conflicting diet sheets. A caregiver may benefit from attending the dietetic appointment so the home plan is understood.

Discuss anaemia and deficiencies with the treating team. Do not assume all anaemia is corrected by the same tablet or that supplements should be started without investigation. Avoid high-dose vitamins, herbal mixtures or products advertised as cancer cures unless the oncology team has reviewed them for safety and interactions.

## Prepare for eating after partial or total gastrectomy

Ask how the operation will change meal size and digestion, and who will guide the gradual return to food. After total gastrectomy, long-term vitamin B12 replacement and monitoring require a plan. Other nutritional problems can also occur, so follow-up should not stop once the wound heals.

Some patients experience symptoms when food passes rapidly into the intestine, sometimes described as dumping symptoms. Ask the dietitian what to watch for and how advice will be adjusted if this happens. Do not impose a rigid internet diet on yourself before you know the operation performed and the symptoms you actually have.

If a feeding tube or another form of nutrition support is a possibility, ask why it may be needed and how it would be managed. It is a support option, not an inevitable feature of every gastrectomy. Arrange training if care will continue outside hospital.

## Medicines, anaesthesia and admission instructions

Bring a current list of prescriptions, blood thinners, diabetes medicines, supplements and allergies. Tell the anaesthesiologist about prior operations, nausea, breathing difficulties, sleep apnoea and heart problems. Explain any side effects from recent cancer treatment and when the last treatment was given.

Gastrectomy usually involves general anaesthesia. Ask about pain relief, monitoring and the plan for breathing, movement and clot prevention. The safest approach depends on your health and the extent of surgery; no article can issue a personalised “cardiac clearance”.

Obtain written instructions for fasting and medicines. A liquid diet or nutritional supplement programme does not automatically satisfy anaesthesia restrictions. Diabetes and reduced intake need a coordinated plan so you are not left deciding independently whether to take or omit treatment.

Do not start bowel cleansing unless the unit has prescribed it for a specific reason. Report any new infection, dehydration or inability to eat before admission. The team may need to reassess rather than proceeding through an unchanged checklist.

## Share a simple recovery record

After discharge, a short record of meals tolerated, vomiting, weight changes and questions can help the dietitian understand a problem. It should support appointments, not become a self-imposed calorie target or a reason to delay seeking help. Ask which changes should be reported immediately and which can be discussed at the planned review. If family members prepare different meals, agree on one written plan so the patient does not receive conflicting restrictions. Include any supplements and prescribed replacement treatments in the handover to the clinician managing care near home.

## Your gastrectomy preparation checklist

### Before the date is finalised

- [ ] Know the tumour location, stage and agreed treatment sequence.
- [ ] Understand whether partial or total gastrectomy is planned.
- [ ] Share endoscopy, biopsy, imaging and treatment records.
- [ ] Discuss weight loss, anaemia and practical dietetic support.
- [ ] Ask about postoperative nutrition and long-term supplementation needs.
- [ ] Obtain a clear medicine and anaesthetic preparation plan.

### Before admission

- [ ] Report bleeding, worsening vomiting, fever or poor fluid intake.
- [ ] Confirm the hospital, reporting time and caregiver contact.
- [ ] Carry identification, prescriptions, insurance approval and relevant images.
- [ ] Follow the prescribed eating, drinking and medicine instructions.
- [ ] Arrange help with meals, shopping and travel after discharge.

### Before returning home

- [ ] Understand the actual operation and any change from the planned procedure.
- [ ] Receive a written food, medicine and activity plan.
- [ ] Learn tube or wound care if required.
- [ ] Confirm pathology, dietetic and oncology appointments.
- [ ] Know the emergency contact route and local review options.

## Recovery and ongoing cancer care

The hospital team monitors pain, circulation, breathing, hydration and how the digestive system is recovering. The timing of food, removal of tubes and discharge depends on progress and the actual operation. A milestone-based plan is more useful than a promise that everyone leaves hospital on a particular day.

Ask what symptoms should prompt early contact. Increasing abdominal pain, fever, persistent vomiting, inability to drink, wound discharge or a swollen painful leg needs assessment. Chest pain, severe breathlessness, collapse or major bleeding requires emergency care. Do not wait for an oncology appointment to report a possible surgical complication.

Final pathology helps the team assess the resection and decide what comes next. Ask when the report will be discussed, whether additional cancer treatment is expected and how nutritional recovery affects that planning. Keep a copy of the pathology and discharge summary for care outside the operating hospital.

## Hospital and financial arrangements in India

Request an estimate that separates surgery, anaesthesia, ward or critical care, investigations and medicines. Ask how feeding support, additional procedures and a longer stay are handled. Chemotherapy, supplements, long-term monitoring and travel may be outside the surgical package.

Confirm insurance authorisation and what records are required. If you will recover in another city, agree who will monitor intake, weight, deficiencies and wound healing. Do not assume every later concern can be managed through a phone call without examination.

## Six mistakes worth preventing

1. **Treating partial and total gastrectomy as the same operation.** Their nutritional implications differ.
2. **Ignoring weight loss because the operation date is near.** Tell the team promptly.
3. **Using restrictive anti-cancer diets.** They can make adequate intake harder.
4. **Assuming a feeding tube is either inevitable or a failure.** Ask about its individual purpose.
5. **Changing diabetes or blood-thinner treatment yourself.** Obtain written coordination.
6. **Stopping dietetic follow-up once eating resumes.** Longer-term deficiencies and symptoms may still need care.

## Questions for your gastric oncology team

1. Where is the cancer, and what is the current stage?
2. Why is this treatment sequence recommended?
3. How much stomach is expected to be removed?
4. Could additional procedures be necessary?
5. What nutrition support do I need before admission?
6. What supplementation or monitoring follows this operation?
7. How will my regular medicines be managed?
8. When will final pathology be available?
9. How will the surgery and oncology teams coordinate follow-up?
10. Which symptoms require urgent assessment?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every stomach cancer need the whole stomach removed?

No. Location, extent and the ability to achieve an appropriate resection influence the operation. Selected very early disease may have other options.

### Why might chemotherapy come before surgery?

It can be part of a planned combined approach. The team should explain the expected purpose, reassessment and intended next steps for your disease.

### Should I eat less to make surgery easier?

Do not restrict food independently. Poor intake and weight loss need assessment, and advice should reflect any obstruction or other symptoms.

### Will I need a feeding tube?

Possibly, but not universally. Ask whether it is anticipated and how it would support your nutrition during recovery.

### Can I still eat normal Indian meals afterwards?

Many familiar foods can be adapted, but portions, timing and tolerance may change. A dietitian can help make the plan practical.

### Are vitamin B12 arrangements important after total gastrectomy?

Yes. Ask for a long-term replacement and monitoring plan rather than relying on a short postoperative prescription.

### Will surgery alone complete treatment?

Not always. Stage, pathology, health and treatment already received influence recommendations for additional therapy.

### What if I keep vomiting after discharge?

Contact the surgical team promptly, especially if fluids cannot be retained. Do not rely on diet changes alone without assessment.

Ask who will organise longer-term blood monitoring and replacement prescriptions if your oncologist and surgeon work at different hospitals. A named clinician helps prevent nutritional follow-up being assumed to belong to someone else.

## Make the nutrition plan part of the operation plan

A gastric cancer surgery checklist should clarify the tumour pathway and prepare for the digestive changes afterwards. Bring the right records, address poor intake early and obtain a workable home plan. Keep pathology, dietetic and oncology review connected so recovery supports the next treatment decision.
