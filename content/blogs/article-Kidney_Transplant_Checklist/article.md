# Kidney Transplant Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Testing for Recipients & Donors

Kidney transplantation brings together two separate safety assessments: whether a recipient can benefit from a transplant and whether a donated kidney can be used safely. When donation is from a living person, that person's health, understanding and freedom to decide need independent attention. A donor's wish to help does not remove the need for a thorough evaluation.

This kidney transplant checklist explains the preparation shared by recipients, donors and families. It covers compatibility tests, general medical assessment, infection screening, practical admission plans and the long-term treatment a recipient must understand before surgery. For Indian patients, it also highlights the need to work through a registered transplant programme and its authorised documentation process.

Transplant assessment is not a promise that surgery will proceed on a particular date. Results may identify problems to treat, a donor may not be suitable, or a deceased-donor offer may need to be declined after further checks. Use this guide to organise your records and questions, while allowing the transplant team to make decisions from current results and the circumstances of both people involved.

## Understand the treatment and the possible pathways

A transplant places a donor kidney into the recipient. The operation is different from dialysis, and it does not remove the need for continuing care. Anti-rejection medicines and regular monitoring are needed for as long as the transplant functions. The balance of benefit and risk depends on the recipient's health, donor factors and available alternatives.

Living-donor transplantation can often be planned in advance once evaluation and permissions are complete. Deceased-donor transplantation depends on an organ becoming available and being suitable. The waiting process, allocation requirements and readiness instructions should be explained by the Indian transplant programme; information from another country cannot establish local waiting-list rules.

Discuss dialysis options and continuing treatment while assessment proceeds. Do not discontinue dialysis or other kidney care because transplantation is being considered. The [NHS Blood and Transplant pathway](https://www.nhsbt.nhs.uk/organ-transplantation/kidney/is-a-kidney-transplant-right-for-you/steps-towards-a-kidney-transplant/) is useful for understanding clinical stages, but its allocation system is not the Indian system.

## Recipient assessment: more than a kidney test

The team reviews the cause of kidney failure, dialysis history, previous operations, blood transfusions, pregnancies and any earlier transplant. Explain heart or vascular disease, diabetes, infections, cancer history, lung problems and functional limitations. Bring records rather than relying on memory when possible.

Some findings require treatment or further assessment before transplantation. The aim is to understand whether you can tolerate surgery and the continuing immunosuppression, and how risks can be reduced. Ask whether an issue means a temporary pause, another test or a different recommendation; these are different outcomes.

| Recipient assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Blood group and tissue typing | Helps assess donor compatibility | During transplant work-up |
| Antibody testing and crossmatch | Assesses immune reaction risk to a particular donor | At stages determined by the transplant team |
| Infection screening | Identifies risks before immunosuppression | According to current transplant protocol and history |
| Heart, lung and vascular assessment | Evaluates fitness and operative considerations | Tests selected from risk, symptoms and planned surgery |
| Cancer screening appropriate to the person | Looks for disease that may change suitability | According to history, age and clinical indications |
| Nutrition and psychosocial review | Identifies support and treatment-adherence needs | As part of a comprehensive assessment |

The [NHSBT testing guide](https://www.nhsbt.nhs.uk/organ-transplantation/kidney/is-a-kidney-transplant-right-for-you/kidney-transplant-tests/) explains blood-group, tissue-type and antibody assessment. Your own centre determines the exact tests and when results need repeating.

## Compatibility: ask what each result means

Blood-group compatibility is only one part of matching. Tissue typing identifies immune-system characteristics, while antibody tests look for potential reactions to donor tissue. Previous transfusion, pregnancy or transplantation can be relevant, so update the team when these occur during the assessment period.

A crossmatch helps assess whether the recipient has a concerning immune reaction to the proposed donor. Do not interpret a result without the transplant specialists: its meaning depends on the testing method and the wider immunological assessment. A result can change the planned pathway even when other tests looked reassuring.

If a living donor and recipient are incompatible, ask what lawful alternatives the programme offers, such as an approved paired-exchange pathway or a selected incompatible-transplant programme. These options have additional criteria and risks. They are not arrangements to organise privately through an intermediary.

## Living donors need their own clinical and consent pathway

A potential donor should have a separate assessment of kidney function, general health, blood pressure, infection risks, anatomy and psychosocial circumstances. The team must consider the donor's health after donation, not merely whether the kidney could work in the recipient.

[NOTTO's living-donor criteria](https://notto.mohfw.gov.in/WriteReadData/Final_sop/Kidney/Kidney_Living_Donor_Criteria.pdf) describe medical and surgical evaluation, compatibility, consent and follow-up. They also emphasise voluntary decisions without pressure or commercialism. Ask how the donor can raise concerns privately and how confidentiality is protected.

The donor should understand the operation, possible short- and long-term risks, recovery needs and alternatives for the recipient. They can change their mind before surgery. A family should not frame donation as an obligation or treat an abnormal test as something to conceal. If the donor is found unsuitable, that finding needs care and explanation in its own right.

### Practical support for the donor

Plan transport, time away from work, childcare and help at home independently of the recipient's arrangements. Both people may be recovering at the same time; one cannot necessarily act as the other's main caregiver. Ask about follow-up for blood pressure, kidney health and general wellbeing after donation.

Clarify which medical costs and follow-up expenses the programme covers and what documents are needed for legitimate reimbursement arrangements. Do not make payments for an organ or use an agent promising to bypass assessment. Questions about rules or financial arrangements belong with the registered programme's authorised team.

## Indian documentation and permissions

Ask the transplant coordinator for the current checklist that applies to your situation, including identity, relationship or other supporting documents and the required authorisation process. Requirements can differ by donor relationship, nationality and applicable rules. Confirm them directly rather than assuming another family's paperwork will be accepted.

Indian organ transplantation is governed by the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues framework. [NOTTO's official information](https://www.dghs.mohfw.gov.in/notto.php) identifies its national coordinating role. Clinical acceptance, administrative authorisation and an agreed operating date are separate steps; ask which remains outstanding.

Keep copies of submissions and confirm that forms reflect the facts accurately. Do not sign blank declarations or share original identity documents with an unverified broker. A hospital's coordinator should explain where documents go and how corrections are made through the proper process.

## Infection, vaccination and dental concerns

Report recent fever, cough, urinary symptoms, skin infection, dental infection or treatment for any infectious illness. Tell the team about exposures or travel relevant to its screening questions. Anti-rejection treatment changes infection risk, so a problem that seems minor may need assessment before transplantation.

Bring vaccination records and ask which vaccines should be reviewed before immunosuppression. Vaccine choices and timing need the transplant team; do not book a live vaccine or another intervention without checking suitability. Ask how household members can support infection prevention without imposing impractical isolation.

Screening results also protect the donor and recipient from transmissible disease. A negative result is not permission to ignore new symptoms later. If illness develops after the assessment is complete, contact the centre and allow it to decide whether repeat testing or postponement is appropriate.

## Medicines, dialysis and anaesthesia planning

List regular medicines, over-the-counter painkillers, herbal products and allergies. Explain blood-thinner treatment and the reason for it. The nephrology, transplant and anaesthesia teams should coordinate instructions for blood pressure, diabetes, dialysis-related treatment and any immunosuppression already prescribed.

Do not alter dialysis timing, fluid restrictions or diet on your own in anticipation of surgery. Ask what to do in the hours before admission and whom to contact if an organ offer arrives near a planned dialysis session. The team may need current blood results or dialysis before proceeding.

Kidney transplantation is usually performed under general anaesthesia. Discuss airway or breathing problems, heart history, previous anaesthesia difficulties and the expected observation area. Fasting instructions must come from the transplant centre, particularly when admission is urgent. Tell staff what you have recently eaten, drunk or taken.

## Prepare to manage medicines after transplantation

Before surgery, understand that anti-rejection medicines cannot be treated as an optional short course. Ask who will teach you the schedule, how prescriptions are renewed and how costs will be managed. Arrange reliable access near home and a way to contact the team if a dose is missed or vomited.

Blood-test timing may be linked to particular medicine doses. Obtain the centre's explicit instructions rather than moving doses or withholding medicines yourself. Ask about interactions, including other prescriptions, supplements and medicines bought without a prescription. Every clinician treating you should know about the transplant.

If remembering treatment has been difficult, discuss reminders, caregiver involvement or other support before discharge. This is a practical care need, not a reason for blame. Agree what to do when travelling, changing routine or running low on medicine so interruptions do not become emergencies.

## Recipient and donor preparation checklist

### During evaluation

- [ ] Understand the recipient's benefits, risks and alternatives.
- [ ] Complete compatibility and health assessments through the transplant programme.
- [ ] Ensure the donor has a private, voluntary consent process.
- [ ] Clarify which permissions and documents apply to the proposed donation.
- [ ] Review infection concerns, vaccination records and nutritional needs.
- [ ] Discuss follow-up costs and access to continuing treatment.

### While waiting or planning a living-donor date

- [ ] Keep the centre informed of transfusions, infections or major health changes.
- [ ] Continue dialysis and prescribed kidney care unless clinicians direct otherwise.
- [ ] Keep contact numbers current and understand the organ-offer instructions.
- [ ] Arrange separate support for donor and recipient.
- [ ] Prepare ID, legal paperwork, prescriptions and recent clinical records.

### At admission and discharge

- [ ] Follow the centre's individual fasting, medicine and dialysis plan.
- [ ] Report current symptoms honestly, even if surgery might be delayed.
- [ ] Learn the post-transplant medicine and laboratory schedule.
- [ ] Confirm urgent contact arrangements and the first follow-up visits.
- [ ] Obtain a separate donor recovery and review plan.

## Early recovery and warning signs

The team monitors urine output, blood results, fluid balance, blood pressure and other recovery measures. Some transplanted kidneys take time to work fully, and dialysis may still be needed temporarily. Ask how progress is being assessed rather than interpreting one urine measurement or blood result in isolation.

Before going home, learn wound care, temperature or blood-pressure monitoring if requested, activity guidance and the appointment schedule. [NHSBT's admission information](https://www.nhsbt.nhs.uk/organ-transplantation/kidney/at-the-transplant-centre/being-admitted-to-the-kidney-transplant-centre/) explains that final checks can still identify reasons not to proceed. A cancelled operation after admission can be a protective decision.

Contact the transplant team promptly for fever, reduced urine, pain over the transplant area, vomiting that prevents medicines or fluids, wound problems or significant illness. Rejection may also be detected through tests rather than obvious symptoms, so attend scheduled monitoring even when you feel well. Severe breathlessness, chest pain or collapse needs emergency care.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Treating blood group as the whole compatibility assessment.** Antibody and tissue-related findings also matter.
2. **Pressuring a donor to proceed.** Independent, voluntary consent is essential.
3. **Assuming authorisation is automatic once tests pass.** Complete the correct legal process through the programme.
4. **Changing dialysis or fluid intake independently.** Current blood chemistry and clinical condition guide preparation.
5. **Budgeting only for the operation.** Continuing medicines and monitoring need a realistic plan.
6. **Ignoring new infections because a date is fixed.** Update the team before admission.
7. **Using unverified brokers or organ payments.** Work only through the authorised transplant pathway.

## Questions for the transplant programme

1. What makes transplantation appropriate for the recipient now?
2. Which medical or compatibility issues remain unresolved?
3. How will the living donor's independent interests be protected?
4. What lawful options exist if this pair is incompatible?
5. Which authorisations and documents are still required?
6. What should change if an infection or transfusion occurs while waiting?
7. Who gives the final dialysis, medicine and fasting instructions?
8. How will ongoing medicines and blood monitoring be arranged?
9. Where should urgent problems be assessed after discharge?
10. What follow-up will the donor receive separately?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a relative automatically a suitable donor?

No. Relationship does not establish medical safety, compatibility or voluntary consent. Each potential donor requires the programme's full independent assessment and applicable permissions.

### Can transplantation proceed if blood groups differ?

Selected programmes may offer specific pathways for some incompatible pairs, but suitability and risks require specialist assessment. Ask about approved options rather than assuming the mismatch is either irrelevant or always final.

### Will my own kidneys usually be removed?

They often remain in place, but there are exceptions based on the underlying disease and other concerns. Ask what is planned for you and why.

### Does a transplant mean no more medicines?

No. Daily anti-rejection treatment and other medicines may be required, with ongoing monitoring. The regimen is adjusted by the transplant team, not stopped when you feel well.

### Can an organ offer be cancelled after I reach hospital?

Yes. New information about your health, compatibility or the donor organ can change the safety assessment. Ask the team to explain the reason and what happens next.

### Should a donor hide a minor health problem to avoid rejection?

No. Accurate disclosure protects both people. A finding may need treatment, further testing or a different decision, and concealment can create avoidable risk.

### Can I miss a review if urine output seems normal?

No. Blood tests and clinical review may identify problems before symptoms appear. Ask about suitable arrangements if distance or cost makes attendance difficult.

### How should families plan recovery support?

Arrange separate help for donor and recipient, including transport, meals, childcare and appointments. Neither person should be assumed able to care fully for the other immediately after surgery.

### Who can explain Indian transplant paperwork?

Use the registered hospital's authorised transplant coordinator and applicable official authorities. This article cannot determine legal eligibility for a particular donor-recipient relationship or nationality.

## Prepare for two people's safety and long-term care

The strongest transplant preparation connects compatibility, medical fitness, voluntary donation and lawful authorisation with dependable follow-up. Keep the programme informed of changes, protect the donor's independence and make ongoing medicine access part of the decision from the beginning.
