# Lung Cancer Surgery Checklist: Pulmonary Function Tests & Thoracic Prep

Lung cancer surgery planning has two separate questions: can the disease be removed appropriately, and how will the remaining lungs and heart support recovery? A scan may show a small tumour, yet breathing tests or lymph-node assessment can still change the recommendation. Conversely, a reduced breathing-test result does not by itself settle the decision without specialist interpretation.

This lung cancer surgery checklist explains staging, pulmonary function tests, anaesthesia discussions and preparation for breathing, pain control and mobility after surgery. It focuses mainly on operations considered for non-small-cell lung cancer, because other lung tumour types can follow different pathways. The proposed operation may remove a segment, a lobe or a larger amount of lung depending on the case.

For patients in India, bring biopsy results, scan images and details of inhalers, oxygen or previous respiratory treatment. Ask whether a thoracic multidisciplinary team has reviewed the plan and how care will continue after discharge. Preparation should improve decision-making and support recovery; it should not become an unsupervised programme that delays treatment or changes prescribed medicines.

## Link the operation to the cancer type and stage

The diagnosis, tumour location, lymph nodes and any spread influence whether surgery is appropriate. Some patients need additional treatment before or after an operation, while others may be offered radiotherapy or another approach. The [NCI non-small-cell lung cancer summary](https://www.cancer.gov/types/lung/hp/non-small-cell-lung-treatment-pdq) describes these stage-dependent options.

Ask whether a tissue diagnosis is established, whether further sampling is needed and what question each proposed test answers. Enlarged lymph nodes or a suspicious scan finding may need clarification before committing to resection. A PET scan, CT or biopsy has a particular role; none should be interpreted in isolation from the whole assessment.

The surgeon should explain the likely extent of removal and why a smaller or larger operation is appropriate. Wedge resection, segmentectomy, lobectomy and pneumonectomy are different procedures. Ask what would make the extent or access method change during surgery.

Video-assisted or robotic surgery describes access to the chest, not the biological behaviour of the cancer. An open approach can be necessary for safety or complete treatment. Do not choose a hospital solely because a technology appears in its advertising.

## Understand pulmonary function testing

Spirometry measures airflow, while a gas-transfer test assesses how effectively gas passes between the lungs and blood. These results help the team estimate the effect of removing lung tissue. They are interpreted with symptoms, exercise capacity and the proposed resection rather than used as an online pass-or-fail score.

The [NICE lung-cancer management guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng122/chapter/Management) discusses spirometry, transfer-factor testing and prediction of postoperative function. Some patients need exercise testing or further respiratory assessment. Ask what the result means for daily activities and the alternatives being considered.

Tell the laboratory about inhalers, recent chest infection, oxygen use and any difficulty completing a test. Follow its preparation instructions. Do not withhold an inhaler independently or force a test when unwell without informing the staff.

## Which investigations may be needed?

| Investigation or assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| CT and appropriate staging imaging | Defines disease extent and operative anatomy | According to the lung-cancer pathway |
| Tissue or lymph-node sampling | Establishes diagnosis or clarifies staging | When the result could change treatment |
| Spirometry and gas-transfer testing | Assesses lung reserve | Before considering curative treatment where relevant |
| Predicted postoperative function | Estimates the effect of planned lung removal | During surgical assessment |
| Exercise testing | Clarifies functional risk | When standard assessment leaves an important question |
| ECG and selected cardiac assessment | Evaluates symptoms or cardiovascular risk | According to history and planned surgery |
| Blood count and relevant blood tests | Identifies anaemia or organ-function concerns | As selected by the treating team |

Bring earlier pulmonary tests and cardiac records. If you have had tuberculosis, COPD, asthma or a previous chest operation, explain the treatment and any current limitations. Do not assume a past diagnosis automatically rules surgery in or out.

## Prepare breathing and physical function safely

Tell the team how far you can walk, whether stairs cause breathlessness and whether this has changed recently. Practical descriptions help more than saying you are “fit for your age”. If breathlessness occurs at rest, worsens suddenly or is accompanied by chest pain, seek prompt assessment.

Ask whether physiotherapy or pulmonary rehabilitation is appropriate before the operation. The [American Lung Association surgery guide](https://www.lung.org/lung-health-diseases/lung-disease-lookup/lung-cancer/treatment/types-of-treatment/lung-cancer-surgery) explains that treatment and recovery vary with the operation and individual health. Any exercise programme should account for symptoms and safety.

Smoking cessation support is worthwhile; tell the team honestly about smoking and other inhaled products. Discuss prescribed inhaler technique and treatment of active respiratory problems. Do not replace inhalers with steam, herbal mixtures or unproven breathing devices.

If appetite is poor or weight is falling, ask for nutritional assessment. Weakness and undernutrition can affect recovery planning even when the lung tests look acceptable. Food advice should consider symptoms, diabetes and the treatment already received.

## Medicines, anaesthesia and pain control

Provide a current list of inhalers, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, diabetes medicines, steroids, supplements and allergies. Tell the anaesthesiologist about sleep apnoea, home oxygen, previous breathing difficulty after anaesthesia and any heart disease. Bring details of recent chemotherapy, immunotherapy or radiotherapy where relevant.

Thoracic surgery generally uses general anaesthesia. The team may need to ventilate the lungs in a particular way during the operation. Ask the anaesthesiologist to explain what this means for your situation and the monitoring planned afterwards.

Pain control supports coughing, deep breathing and movement. Discuss the options, prior pain-medicine reactions and how side effects will be managed. Do not assume that needing pain relief means a patient should remain in bed; ask how the team balances comfort with safe mobilisation.

Get written medicine and fasting instructions. Blood-thinner interruption must be coordinated with the clinician responsible for the original prescription, particularly after a coronary stent or clot. Do not stop inhalers or steroids independently. Tell staff about new fever, cough or sputum changes before admission.

## Plan for chest drains and postoperative support

A chest drain may be used to remove air or fluid while the lung and chest recover. Ask how the team assesses when it can be removed and whether going home with a drain is ever part of the pathway. Do not pull, clamp or disconnect it yourself.

Discuss what help you may need with shopping, transport, stairs and heavier tasks. If you already use oxygen, ask how supply and equipment will be handled. Needing oxygen before or after surgery requires individual assessment, not an assumption that it will be permanent.

Arrange a smoke-free environment and practical support for appointments. Ask which service can assess urgent symptoms near home, especially if the operation is in another state. A travel plan should allow for chest review and the treating team's advice about flying or long journeys.

## Turn the recovery plan into practical arrangements

Ask the physiotherapist to demonstrate any breathing or supported-cough technique the unit wants you to use. Check that you can perform it comfortably and understand how pain relief fits around movement. Buying a breathing device without instruction is not a substitute for learning the technique or reporting increasing breathlessness. If hearing, language or visual difficulties make instructions hard to follow, request a format you and your caregiver can use.

Discuss the layout of your home before discharge planning is complete. A bedroom reached only by several flights of stairs, limited access to a toilet or the need to carry water can affect the support required. Ask whether a temporary arrangement or community rehabilitation input is appropriate. If family support is limited, tell the team early rather than promising help that will not be available.

For travel from another state, make a list of the records to carry: operation details, pathology when ready, discharge prescription and any oxygen or drain instructions. Confirm which clinician will reassess breathing locally and how to contact the thoracic service. A hospital estimate should also explain whether later drain care, rehabilitation and specialist reviews are included or billed separately. These practical details help prevent gaps in care without making assumptions about how quickly every patient will recover.

## Lung surgery preparation checklist

### Before admission is confirmed

- [ ] Understand the cancer type, stage and multidisciplinary recommendation.
- [ ] Know the expected amount of lung removal and access method.
- [ ] Review pulmonary-function results with the thoracic team.
- [ ] Discuss inhalers, smoking cessation, nutrition and safe activity.
- [ ] Share previous chest treatment and current oncology records.
- [ ] Obtain a coordinated medicine, fasting and pain-relief plan.

### Before and on admission

- [ ] Report new infection symptoms or worsening breathlessness.
- [ ] Carry scans, pathology, prescriptions and insurance documents.
- [ ] Bring prescribed respiratory equipment if the hospital requests it.
- [ ] Confirm reporting time, caregiver contact and transport arrangements.
- [ ] Tell staff about medicine changes or accidental eating and drinking.

### Before leaving hospital

- [ ] Understand wound, drain and breathing instructions.
- [ ] Know the activity and pain-medicine plan.
- [ ] Confirm pathology review and further oncology appointments.
- [ ] Ask when travel, driving and work can be reconsidered.
- [ ] Obtain an urgent contact route for breathing or wound problems.

## Recovery and concerning symptoms

The team monitors breathing, circulation, pain, mobility and drain function. Supported coughing and movement are often part of recovery, but the pace is adjusted to the patient. Ask what progress is expected and which milestones must be reached before discharge.

Seek emergency care for sudden severe breathlessness, chest pain, collapse or significant coughing of blood. Fever, worsening cough, increasing wound redness or discharge, a painful swollen leg or a clear deterioration in exercise tolerance needs prompt assessment. Do not dismiss new symptoms simply because some breathlessness was expected after resection.

Keep the pathology appointment. Findings from the removed tissue and lymph nodes can influence the next treatment recommendation. Ask for a written summary that explains the operation performed, the final stage when available and who coordinates further therapy.

## Six common mistakes to avoid

1. **Reading one lung-test number as an automatic verdict.** Ask for integrated assessment.
2. **Confusing robotic access with a different cancer stage.** Technology does not replace staging.
3. **Withholding inhalers without instructions.** Breathing treatment needs coordination.
4. **Ignoring a new chest infection before admission.** Report changes early.
5. **Staying immobile because movement is uncomfortable.** Ask for adequate pain relief and safe support.
6. **Booking flights before chest review.** Obtain specific travel advice.

## Questions for the thoracic team

1. What cancer type and stage are currently established?
2. Do lymph nodes need further assessment?
3. What extent of lung removal is planned, and why?
4. What do my predicted postoperative breathing results mean?
5. Are non-surgical treatments reasonable alternatives?
6. How should my inhalers and other medicines be managed?
7. What pain relief and respiratory support are planned?
8. Might I go home with oxygen or a drain?
9. When will pathology and additional treatment be discussed?
10. Which symptoms require immediate help after discharge?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a small lung tumour always mean surgery is suitable?

No. Cancer type, stage, anatomy and overall fitness all matter. A thoracic team should interpret these together.

### Can reduced lung function rule out every curative option?

Not automatically. The planned operation, exercise capacity and alternatives need specialist assessment. Do not self-exclude using a single result.

### Why are lymph nodes investigated before surgery?

Their involvement can change staging and treatment recommendations. Ask what a proposed sample would clarify.

### Is removing a lobe the same as removing a lung?

No. A lobectomy removes one lobe; pneumonectomy removes an entire lung. Other smaller resections may be appropriate in selected cases.

### Should I stop my inhaler before breathing tests?

Follow the testing laboratory's instructions and confirm uncertainty with the team. Do not alter treatment independently.

### Will I definitely need oxygen at home?

No. Oxygen needs are assessed individually. Ask how the team will decide and arrange supply if required.

### Can I travel by air soon after discharge?

Only after appropriate advice from the thoracic team. Healing, chest findings and any drain or air leak can affect safety.

### Will surgery be my only cancer treatment?

It may be, but some patients need treatment before or after surgery. The final plan depends on staging, pathology and the wider oncology assessment.

If your job involves dust, fumes or physical exertion, discuss these exposures when planning return to work. A task-specific plan may require a different review from a mainly seated role.

## Use preparation to understand your breathing reserve

A lung cancer surgery checklist should make staging, lung function and recovery support understandable. Ask what the tests mean for your proposed operation, coordinate medicines and report respiratory changes early. Plan follow-up and urgent access before leaving the centre so postoperative care remains connected.
