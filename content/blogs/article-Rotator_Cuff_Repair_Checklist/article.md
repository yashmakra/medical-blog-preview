# Rotator Cuff Repair Checklist: Pre-Op Physical Therapy & Hospital Prep

Rotator cuff repair aims to reattach a torn shoulder tendon when surgery is the appropriate option. Its preparation should connect the scan findings with your symptoms, the condition of the tendon and the rehabilitation you can realistically follow. A visible tear does not automatically mean an operation, and a short arthroscopic admission does not mean that the tendon is ready for normal loading immediately afterwards.

This rotator cuff surgery checklist helps you discuss those decisions before booking. It explains the role of examination and imaging, when preoperative physiotherapy may help and why the postoperative sling and exercise plan need to be understood in advance. It also covers medicines, anaesthesia and practical arrangements for patients in India.

Tell the surgeon whether the problem followed a recent injury or developed gradually, which arm you rely on and what activities you need to recover. Work, caring responsibilities and access to physiotherapy can affect preparation. Use the checklist to make the plan specific to your shoulder; do not begin forceful exercises or change medicines based on a generic repair programme.

## Decide whether repair is the right treatment

The rotator cuff is a group of muscles and tendons that helps control shoulder movement. Tears vary in size, depth, location and tissue quality. Pain and weakness also need examination because neck problems, arthritis or other conditions can contribute to symptoms.

Some patients improve with non-operative care such as an appropriate physiotherapy programme and symptom treatment. Others may be advised to consider surgery, including after particular injuries or persistent functional problems. The [AAOS 2025 rotator cuff guideline](https://www.aaos.org/rccpg2025) recognises benefit from both physiotherapy and surgery in selected small-to-medium full-thickness tears; the choice should reflect the individual case.

Ask whether the tear appears repairable and what limits the expected result. Tendon retraction, muscle condition and other shoulder changes can affect options. If repair is unsuitable, the surgeon may discuss a different procedure; shoulder replacement is a separate pathway rather than another name for cuff repair.

### Understand the proposed procedure

Repair commonly involves securing tendon back to bone with sutures and anchors. The operation may be arthroscopic or use another approach. Ask whether treatment of the biceps tendon or another structure is also planned and what findings could change the procedure.

Discuss relevant risks, including infection, stiffness, ongoing symptoms and failure of the tendon to heal or a later retear. Do not accept a guarantee of complete strength or return to a particular sport. The [AAOS surgical-options information](https://www.orthoinfo.org/treatment/rotator-cuff-tears-surgical-treatment-options/) explains the repair concept and the importance of rehabilitation.

## Examination and investigations

Bring original images as well as reports and tell the team about earlier injections, physiotherapy, operations or dislocations. Describe weakness after injury, night pain, stiffness and numbness. If arm function has suddenly changed, seek assessment rather than waiting to finish an arbitrary exercise course.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder examination | Relates pain, movement and weakness to clinical findings | At the specialist assessment |
| X-rays | Evaluates bone, joint changes and alternative explanations | When relevant to the diagnosis and plan |
| Ultrasound or MRI | Defines tendon tear and associated soft-tissue findings | According to the clinical question and available imaging |
| Selected medical tests | Reviews anaesthesia and health risks | Based on illness, medicines and the planned operation |
| Additional assessment | Clarifies neck, nerve or other contributing problems | When symptoms suggest another cause |

Ask whether a scan finding explains the limitation you want treated. Imaging is important, but the decision should not rely on a report phrase alone. Routine blood tests also need a reason; [NICE NG45](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng45/chapter/recommendations) supports selection according to the patient and operation.

## Preoperative physiotherapy should have a purpose

Ask whether the aim before surgery is maintaining comfortable motion, improving a particular movement or learning postoperative techniques. The recommendation may differ for a recent traumatic tear, a stiff shoulder or a longstanding degenerative problem. Do not assume that every patient must complete the same prehabilitation course.

Tell the therapist which movements cause pain and what exercises you have already tried. Avoid forceful stretching or heavy resistance that has not been approved. Ask how to stay active without overloading the shoulder and whether work tasks need temporary adjustment while awaiting treatment.

Use the appointment to learn practical skills as well as exercises: putting on a sling, protecting the arm while dressing and using the hand without moving the repaired shoulder beyond the prescribed limits. Written instructions should match the surgeon's planned repair and be updated if operative findings change it.

## Medicines and the anaesthesia discussion

Give the team all prescriptions, pain medicines, diabetes treatment, blood thinners, supplements and allergies. Report recent steroid injections and their dates. Ask whether any treatment affects the operation date or healing plan; do not discontinue a prescribed medicine or arrange another injection without checking.

The anaesthetic may include general anaesthesia and a regional nerve block. Ask about the purpose of a block, temporary numbness, arm protection and pain relief as it wears off. Explain breathing disease, sleep apnoea and existing nerve symptoms so the anaesthesiologist can discuss relevant precautions.

Follow individual fasting instructions and clarify approved day-of-surgery medicines. Arrange the required escort and supervision, even if day-case surgery is planned. A comfortable first evening is easier to manage when prescriptions, food and transport have been organised before admission.

## Make rehabilitation possible at home

Ask how long the sling may be required and which movements are initially allowed. Tendon healing and exercise progression cannot be judged by pain alone. A therapist may help distinguish assisted movement, active movement and strengthening, which are different stages of rehabilitation.

Plan help with shopping, cooking, laundry, childcare and personal care. Loose front-opening clothing and accessible storage may reduce awkward reaching. If you use a walking aid or push through your arms to stand, discuss safe alternatives before surgery. Do not assume the unoperated arm can manage every task.

Arrange physiotherapy near home and confirm how the local therapist will receive the operative plan. For patients travelling within India, include early reviews and transport in the estimate of time and cost. Ask how progress will be reassessed if pain, stiffness or access problems make the programme difficult.

## Rotator cuff repair checklist

### Before choosing surgery

- [ ] Understand how examination and imaging explain the symptoms.
- [ ] Compare suitable non-operative treatment and surgical options.
- [ ] Clarify repairability and any additional planned procedures.
- [ ] Discuss realistic goals for work, sleep and activities.
- [ ] Review recent injections, medicines and relevant health conditions.

### Before admission

- [ ] Learn the purpose and limits of any preoperative exercises.
- [ ] Arrange home support, physiotherapy and an escort.
- [ ] Obtain fasting, medicine and anaesthesia instructions.
- [ ] Bring ID, scan images, prescriptions and insurance papers.
- [ ] Report fever, skin problems near the shoulder or a new illness.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Practise sling use and permitted daily activities.
- [ ] Obtain the written repair-specific exercise plan.
- [ ] Confirm pain relief, wound care and review appointments.
- [ ] Know which symptoms require urgent assessment.

## Recovery and when to seek help

The team checks comfort, circulation, sensation and wound condition. Follow instructions for dressings, sling removal and any permitted hand, wrist or elbow movement. Do not begin shoulder strengthening because the skin wounds look healed; the tendon and its attachment need their own recovery plan.

Contact the team for increasing wound redness or drainage, fever, severe worsening pain, unexpected persistent numbness or a new injury to the operated arm. A cold or discoloured hand or serious breathing difficulty needs urgent assessment. If a fall or sudden pull occurs, explain what happened rather than testing the repair repeatedly yourself.

## Six mistakes to avoid

1. **Assuming every MRI tear needs repair.** Relate imaging to symptoms, goals and alternatives.
2. **Delaying assessment of sudden post-injury weakness.** Ask for clinical review rather than self-managing indefinitely.
3. **Copying an exercise programme from another repair.** Tear size and operative details can change restrictions.
4. **Using early pain relief as permission to lift.** A nerve block or improved comfort does not establish tendon healing.
5. **Failing to disclose injections or smoking.** The surgeon needs information relevant to timing and healing.
6. **Booking surgery without rehabilitation access.** Arrange a realistic follow-up pathway first.

## Questions for your shoulder specialist

1. Which tendon is torn, and how does it explain my symptoms?
2. Is the tear repairable, and what limits the expected benefit?
3. What non-operative options remain reasonable?
4. What is the purpose of physiotherapy before surgery?
5. Is treatment of the biceps or another structure planned?
6. How will the repair influence sling and movement restrictions?
7. Who will supervise exercise progression near my home?
8. What should I do after a fall, sudden pain or difficulty following rehabilitation?
9. When will work, driving and sports decisions be reviewed?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can physiotherapy heal every torn tendon?

It may improve pain and function, but that is different from proving that a structural tear has healed. Discuss what improvement is realistic and when reassessment is appropriate.

### Does a larger tear always mean a worse result?

Size is one factor, alongside tissue quality, muscle condition, stiffness, health and goals. Ask the surgeon to explain your overall prognosis without reducing it to one measurement.

### Must I do strengthening before the operation?

Not automatically. The therapist and surgeon should specify suitable preparation for your tear. Forceful resistance can be inappropriate for some patients.

### Is an anchor the same as a shoulder replacement?

No. An anchor helps secure tendon to bone; replacement changes joint surfaces. Ask which implants or additional procedures are actually included in your operation.

### Can I remove the sling when pain improves?

Only according to your team's instructions. The sling may protect the repair even when discomfort is settling, and exercise permission should be explicit.

### When can I drive after repair?

Ask about sling use, control of the arm, pain medicines and the ability to manage an emergency manoeuvre. The decision also needs to respect clinical restrictions and insurance requirements.

### Does a sudden twinge mean the tendon has torn again?

Not necessarily, but report a significant event or new loss of function. A clinician should assess the situation; repeated self-testing can make the problem harder to manage.

### What if recovery seems slower than someone else's?

Compare progress with your own repair-specific plan. Tear characteristics, associated procedures and general health differ, so the team should review concerns rather than promise a shared timetable.

## Protect the repair while rebuilding function

The useful preparation is a clear decision about surgery and a feasible rehabilitation plan. Understand the scan, disclose relevant treatment and practise practical tasks before admission. After surgery, follow the repair-specific progression and ask for review when symptoms or circumstances change.
