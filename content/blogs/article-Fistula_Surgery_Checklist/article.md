# Fistula Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Clearance & Anorectal Procedure Prep

An anal fistula is an abnormal tunnel between the anal canal and nearby skin, often following an abscess. Repeated swelling or drainage can be frustrating, but the operation should be chosen from the tract's anatomy and its relationship to the muscles that control stool. The small external opening does not reveal how much muscle is involved beneath it.

This fistula surgery checklist helps you prepare for a colorectal assessment, understand why imaging or examination under anaesthesia may be needed and discuss options that protect continence. It also covers medicine instructions, wound care and practical support after treatment in India. A seton, fistulotomy and other fistula procedures have different aims; they should not be treated as interchangeable package names.

Tell the surgeon about earlier abscess drainage, previous repairs, bowel symptoms and any difficulty controlling gas or stool. If there is a painful swelling with fever or you feel unwell, seek prompt assessment rather than waiting for an elective surgery date. Preparation starts with controlling infection and understanding the problem, not selecting a marketed technique in advance.

## Map the fistula before choosing treatment

The assessment considers the internal opening, tract, possible branches and sphincter involvement. An abscess is an infected collection; a fistula is the continuing tract. They may occur together, but drainage of an abscess and definitive fistula treatment are not always the same operation.

The [ASCRS fistula information](https://fascrs.org/Web/Patients/Diseases-and-Conditions/A-Z/Abscess-and-Fistula-Expanded-Information.aspx) explains that anatomy influences treatment and that preserving continence matters. Ask whether the fistula is considered simple or complex and what that means for your plan.

A fistulotomy opens a suitable tract to heal from its base. When important sphincter muscle is involved, a draining seton or another sphincter-preserving approach may be considered. A seton can provide controlled drainage and may be part of staged care rather than an immediate cure. Ask what the intended next step is.

## Assessment and investigations

Describe pain, drainage, swelling and any episodes when the opening appears to close. Bring previous operative notes and scan images, because earlier treatment can affect anatomy. Explain diarrhoea, abdominal symptoms, Crohn's disease, diabetes and any immune-suppressing treatment. These can change investigation and management.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical anorectal examination | Looks for openings, drainage and other disease | During colorectal assessment |
| Pelvic MRI or another specialist imaging test | Maps branches, collections or complex anatomy | Selected recurrent, complex or otherwise uncertain cases |
| Examination under anaesthesia | Allows assessment and sometimes treatment | When needed to define the tract or manage symptoms |
| Bowel investigation | Assesses a possible underlying condition | Symptoms or history suggest it, not for every fistula |
| Selected blood tests | Evaluates infection, diabetes or operative risk | According to illness and the proposed procedure |

Not every fistula needs MRI. Ask which uncertainty the test addresses. Baseline continence should be discussed sensitively: difficulty controlling gas or stool is relevant information, not an embarrassing detail to omit.

### If Crohn's disease is involved

Fistula management may require coordination between gastroenterology and colorectal surgery. Medicines for the underlying disease can be important, while surgery may address infection and drainage. Do not assume that a standard tract-cutting operation is appropriate for every Crohn's-related fistula.

## Consent, medicines and anaesthesia

Ask what is planned if the tract is different from expected at examination. The surgeon may choose drainage or a seton rather than proceeding with a more definitive operation. Agree the possible choices and their purposes before anaesthesia where possible.

Ask how your progress will be judged between stages. Record whether the immediate aim is relieving infection, keeping the tract draining or attempting closure. This distinction helps you understand continuing discharge and makes follow-up discussions more useful than asking only whether the skin opening has disappeared.

If work involves prolonged driving or limited toilet access, discuss that before admission. Ask whether a temporary change in duties would make dressing care and review appointments more manageable while the treated area heals.

Provide details of blood thinners, diabetes medicines, steroids, biologic treatments, painkillers and supplements. The relevant prescribers and surgical team should coordinate any adjustment. Do not stop immune treatment or start antibiotics yourself to make the operation “safer”.

Anaesthesia may be general, regional or another suitable technique depending on the procedure. Discuss previous problems, pain relief and discharge arrangements. Follow written fasting instructions. An enema or other bowel preparation should be used only if specifically prescribed; a full bowel cleanse is not automatically necessary for every fistula procedure.

## Prepare for drainage and wound care

Ask whether an open wound, dressing or seton is expected and how to keep the area clean. Arrange toilet access, appropriate absorbent pads if recommended and clothing that does not rub. Ask who can teach a caregiver to assist without disturbing a seton or dressing.

Discuss stool consistency, fluid advice and any prescribed bowel medicines. Diarrhoea and constipation may each make care difficult, but the solution must fit the underlying condition and operation. Do not insert creams or antiseptics into the tract or attempt to tighten, cut or remove a seton.

Take ID, prescriptions, insurance papers, scans and previous surgical records. Confirm whether staged treatment, dressings and review appointments are included in the estimate. Arrange transport and an escort according to the anaesthesia instructions.

## Practical fistula surgery checklist

### Before the plan is finalised

- [ ] Explain previous abscesses, procedures and current drainage.
- [ ] Discuss bowel disease and baseline continence.
- [ ] Understand the tract anatomy and reason for the proposed technique.
- [ ] Clarify whether drainage or staged treatment may be necessary.
- [ ] Agree a medicine plan with the relevant clinicians.

### Before admission

- [ ] Report fever, a new painful swelling or significant deterioration.
- [ ] Follow only the bowel and fasting instructions issued to you.
- [ ] Carry scan files, operation notes, medicine details and documents.
- [ ] Arrange transport, an escort and suitable toilet access at home.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Learn the wound or seton care instructions.
- [ ] Confirm expected drainage and symptoms needing urgent review.
- [ ] Know the follow-up date and any proposed second stage.

## Recovery and warning signs

Healing depends on the tract, technique and underlying disease. Drainage may continue while an open wound heals or a draining seton remains in place. Ask what is expected rather than judging success solely by whether the skin opening has closed.

Seek prompt assessment for fever, increasing swelling, severe or worsening pain, spreading redness, substantial bleeding, inability to pass urine or feeling significantly unwell. A reduction in drainage accompanied by rising pain can warrant review rather than celebration. Ask how to obtain urgent colorectal care out of hours.

## Five mistakes to avoid

1. **Judging the tract from the skin opening.** Deeper anatomy determines treatment risk.
2. **Hiding continence problems.** They can influence a safer choice of procedure.
3. **Assuming a seton means treatment failed.** It may be a planned drainage stage.
4. **Waiting with fever and a painful swelling.** An abscess may need urgent assessment.
5. **Accepting a guaranteed cure claim.** Recurrence risk varies and should be discussed honestly.

## Questions for your colorectal surgeon

1. Where does the tract run in relation to the sphincter?
2. Is imaging needed, and what would it change?
3. Is there an abscess requiring drainage first?
4. Why is the proposed operation appropriate for my continence risk?
5. Could a seton or staged procedure be needed?
6. Does bowel disease alter the treatment plan?
7. How will I manage dressings and bowel movements?
8. How will healing, recurrence and continence be assessed?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a fistula the same as piles?

No. Piles involve haemorrhoidal tissue; a fistula is an abnormal tract. Pain, bleeding or drainage should be examined rather than diagnosed from a symptom label.

### Does every fistula need an MRI?

No. The surgeon selects imaging when it helps clarify anatomy or another concern, especially in more complex or recurrent disease.

### Can antibiotics alone cure the tract?

They may be needed for particular infection-related situations, but they do not usually resolve an established ordinary anal fistula. Underlying Crohn's disease requires a different coordinated treatment discussion.

### Why is the surgeon reluctant to cut the whole tract?

Dividing too much sphincter muscle can affect control of stool. Ask how the proposed technique balances healing with preserving function.

### Can I work with a draining seton?

Often daily activities can be adapted, but comfort, drainage and the job matter. Ask about sitting, physical work and dressing arrangements for your situation.

### Should I remove the seton when drainage stops?

No. Only the treating team should decide its management. Contact the unit if it breaks, falls out or becomes troublesome.

### Is a second operation always a complication?

No. Staged treatment may be intentional. Clarify the reason for each stage and what findings determine whether another procedure is needed.

### How will I know the fistula has healed?

The team assesses symptoms and the treated area, with further tests when indicated. Skin closure alone does not always establish healing of the deeper problem.

## Prepare around anatomy and continence

The most useful checklist connects accurate mapping with a clear treatment aim, safe medicines and manageable wound care. Know whether treatment is staged and keep follow-up even if drainage improves. Protecting continence and controlling infection deserve as much attention as closing the tract.
