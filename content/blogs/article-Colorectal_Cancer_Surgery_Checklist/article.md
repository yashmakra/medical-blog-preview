# Colorectal Cancer Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Bowel Prep & Stoma Planning

Before colorectal cancer surgery, the most useful question is not simply “Have I completed all my tests?” It is “Do I understand the treatment plan for my cancer?” A tumour in the colon and one low in the rectum can require different imaging, treatment sequences and decisions about joining the bowel or creating a stoma. Preparation should reflect that distinction from the first consultation.

This colorectal cancer surgery checklist explains staging, nutrition, bowel-preparation instructions, stoma counselling and the practical steps before admission. It also describes the recovery discussions that should happen beforehand, including changes in bowel function and the possibility of further cancer treatment. It does not assume that every patient needs immediate surgery or the same bowel-cleaning regimen.

For patients in India, bring biopsy reports, colonoscopy details and the actual imaging files, and ask whether a multidisciplinary team has reviewed the case. Plan access to pathology results, a stoma nurse where relevant and follow-up near home. Your surgeon and oncology team should explain how each preparation step supports the specific operation being proposed.

## Establish the site, stage and sequence of treatment

The colon and rectum are parts of the large bowel, but their cancer pathways are not identical. Surgery commonly removes the affected segment with associated lymph nodes. The remaining bowel may be joined, or a stoma may be needed temporarily or permanently. The exact plan depends on cancer location, extent, bowel condition and the patient's health.

A diagnosis usually rests on endoscopy and pathology, with imaging used to assess spread. Ask what is already known, which findings remain uncertain and whether further tests would change treatment. Do not infer the stage from the size of a lesion seen in a photograph or from a tumour-marker result alone.

For rectal cancer, treatment before surgery may be recommended according to the stage and multidisciplinary assessment. Other patients may follow different pathways. The [NCI rectal-cancer treatment summary](https://www.cancer.gov/types/colorectal/hp/rectal-treatment-pdq) demonstrates why rectal disease should not be treated as a simple extension of a generic colon-surgery plan.

Ask whether the aim is cure, symptom control or another defined goal. If there is obstruction, perforation or significant bleeding, urgent management can differ from an elective pathway. Severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, marked distension or inability to pass stool or wind requires prompt clinical assessment; do not start bowel cleansing while waiting for advice.

## Understand the operation and the possibility of a changed plan

The surgeon should name the planned resection and explain whether a laparoscopic, robotic or open approach is intended. The access method does not determine the cancer stage or guarantee a particular outcome. Conversion to open surgery can be appropriate when safety or cancer clearance requires it.

Ask how the tumour will be localised during the operation and whether an additional endoscopic step is needed. If another organ may be involved, discuss whether additional specialists or procedures are anticipated. Consent should cover meaningful possibilities without suggesting that every complication is inevitable.

The [NICE colorectal-cancer guideline](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng151/chapter/Recommendations) highlights discussion of possible treatment changes, stoma formation and postoperative bowel, urinary and sexual effects. Ask which of these issues is particularly relevant to your operation, especially for lower rectal surgery.

## Preoperative investigations: bring the records, not just a checklist

Carry biopsy and histology reports, colonoscopy findings, CT or MRI images and reports, previous abdominal operation notes and recent prescriptions. If testing was done at another centre, ask how the images and slides can be transferred for review.

| Investigation or assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Endoscopy and tissue diagnosis | Confirms the lesion and pathological findings | According to the diagnostic pathway |
| CT staging | Assesses spread and helps plan treatment | As directed by the colorectal oncology team |
| Pelvic MRI | Defines local rectal-cancer anatomy | Particularly relevant to rectal cancer |
| Blood count and iron-related assessment | Identifies anaemia requiring a treatment plan | According to bleeding, symptoms and blood results |
| Kidney function and electrolytes | Informs fluid, contrast and medicine planning | According to comorbidity and preparation needs |
| Nutrition and functional assessment | Identifies weight loss, weakness and support needs | Before major bowel surgery, especially with poor intake |

Some tests establish the cancer plan; others assess fitness for the operation. Ask who has reviewed each result and whether an abnormality needs action. A completed laboratory report is not the same as a completed clinical assessment.

## Nutrition, anaemia and activity before surgery

Tell the team if you are losing weight, eating less, avoiding food because of symptoms or struggling to afford suitable meals. Ask for dietetic advice rather than starting a restrictive “anti-cancer diet”. Nutritional preparation must account for any narrowing or obstruction of the bowel and the planned operation.

Anaemia can have several causes, including bleeding, and should be assessed rather than treated with unprescribed iron or repeated transfusions by assumption. Ask what correction is appropriate and whether it affects timing. Do not independently delay cancer treatment to reach a weight, haemoglobin or fitness target.

Discuss safe activity, breathing practice and smoking cessation with the team. A tailored prehabilitation plan may help some patients prepare, but strenuous exercise is inappropriate if symptoms or comorbidities make it unsafe. Describe what you can actually manage, such as walking to the bathroom or climbing stairs, so advice is realistic.

## Bowel preparation and fasting are separate instructions

Elective colorectal pathways may include mechanical bowel preparation and prescribed oral antibiotics, but the exact regimen depends on the procedure and the treating service. The [ASCRS/SAGES enhanced-recovery guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9839829/) discusses bowel preparation alongside nutrition, recovery milestones and stoma education. It should not be converted into a self-prescription.

Ask for written instructions stating the product, dose, timing, allowed fluids and what to do if you vomit or cannot complete it. Diabetes, kidney disease, dehydration and suspected obstruction need special consideration. Do not reuse a colonoscopy preparation because it is already available at home.

Fasting for anaesthesia is a separate plan. Clarify the cut-off for food and permitted drinks, and whether any carbohydrate drink is specifically prescribed. Do not assume that completing bowel preparation means it is safe to drink freely until admission. Report accidental intake honestly.

## Medicines and anaesthesia planning

Bring details of anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, diabetes drugs, steroids, pain medicines and supplements. Ask for a named plan for each relevant medicine, including when and by whom it will be restarted. Do not stop a blood thinner or alter insulin without the responsible clinicians' instructions.

Major colorectal surgery generally uses general anaesthesia. Discuss previous anaesthetic problems, sleep apnoea, heart or lung disease and the proposed pain-relief options. Ask how nausea, breathing exercises, clot prevention and mobilisation will be managed. Devices or injections used for clot prevention must be prescribed for your circumstances.

Tell the team about recent chemotherapy, radiotherapy or significant treatment side effects. Scheduling should account for cancer treatment, blood results, infection and recovery rather than a fixed interval copied from another patient's experience.

## Stoma preparation should happen before admission where possible

A stoma brings part of the bowel to an opening on the abdomen so stool can enter a pouch. Its purpose and expected duration depend on the operation. A temporary stoma is not an absolute promise of reversal; later decisions depend on healing, health and the cancer plan.

If a stoma is possible, ask to meet a trained stoma professional for counselling and site assessment where feasible. Discuss clothing, eyesight, hand function, skin problems, work and religious or daily routines that may affect pouch care. These are practical care needs, not reasons to hide concerns.

Ask about access to supplies after returning home and who can help if the pouch leaks or skin becomes sore. If an ileostomy is planned, obtain specific advice about output, hydration and when to call. Do not apply a generic fluid target without considering your stoma and other medical conditions.

## Admission and discharge checklist

### Before the surgery date

- [ ] Understand the tumour site, staging findings and agreed treatment sequence.
- [ ] Confirm the operation, alternatives and possible stoma.
- [ ] Share original pathology, endoscopy and scan records.
- [ ] Discuss weight loss, anaemia and practical support needs.
- [ ] Receive medicine, bowel-preparation and fasting instructions.
- [ ] Arrange stoma counselling if relevant.

### The day before and on admission

- [ ] Follow only the prescribed bowel-preparation regimen.
- [ ] Report vomiting, inability to drink, fever or worsening abdominal symptoms.
- [ ] Carry identification, prescriptions, reports and insurance approval.
- [ ] Confirm the correct hospital, reporting time and caregiver contact.
- [ ] Tell staff the last doses of important medicines and any accidental intake.

### Before leaving hospital

- [ ] Understand the actual operation and any change from the original plan.
- [ ] Obtain a wound, diet, mobility and medicine plan.
- [ ] Learn stoma care and supply arrangements if applicable.
- [ ] Confirm pathology review and the next oncology appointment.
- [ ] Know the urgent contact route for pain, fever, vomiting or dehydration.

## Recovery and results are part of treatment

Enhanced-recovery care may involve early supported movement, breathing exercises and a planned return to drinking and food. Your team decides what is suitable after the actual operation. A drain, catheter or temporary change in bowel habit does not by itself establish that something has gone wrong.

Seek prompt assessment for increasing abdominal pain, fever, persistent vomiting, inability to maintain fluids, wound discharge or a painful swollen leg. Severe breathlessness, chest pain, collapse or heavy bleeding needs emergency care. After a bowel join, deterioration should not be dismissed because a previous day seemed comfortable.

Ask when the final pathology will be discussed. It can refine the stage, assess margins and lymph nodes and influence further treatment. Keep copies of the operation and discharge summaries so care can continue smoothly between surgical and oncology teams.

If you have a stoma, ask the nurse to check that you can obtain the correct pouching supplies near home. Demonstrating a change before discharge and receiving a written supply list can make the transition to another city's care much easier.

## Six mistakes to avoid

1. **Treating colon and rectal surgery as identical.** Their staging and treatment sequences can differ.
2. **Starting bowel cleansing during obstructive symptoms.** Obtain urgent advice first.
3. **Leaving stoma questions until discharge.** Practical preparation is easier beforehand.
4. **Restricting food despite weight loss.** Seek tailored nutrition support.
5. **Stopping medicines without a coordinated plan.** Bleeding and clot risks both matter.
6. **Missing the pathology appointment.** The next cancer-treatment decision may depend on it.

## Questions for the colorectal team

1. Where is the tumour, and what is the current stage?
2. Has the multidisciplinary team agreed the treatment sequence?
3. Why is this operation recommended?
4. Could the access method or operation change?
5. What is the chance of needing a stoma in my case?
6. What bowel preparation is prescribed and why?
7. How will nutrition and anaemia be addressed?
8. Which bowel, urinary or sexual effects should I discuss?
9. When will pathology and further treatment be reviewed?
10. Who should we contact after discharge?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every patient have surgery first?

No. Cancer site, stage and multidisciplinary assessment determine the sequence. Some rectal cancers need treatment before an operation.

### Is a stoma always permanent?

No. It may be temporary or permanent depending on the operation. Ask what would need to happen before reversal could be considered.

### Is bowel preparation the same as fasting?

No. One prepares the bowel; the other addresses anaesthetic safety. Obtain both sets of instructions and ask how they fit together.

### Can I use leftover colonoscopy laxatives?

Only if the surgical team specifically prescribes that product and regimen. Do not substitute preparations yourself.

### Will keyhole surgery remove less cancer?

The intended cancer resection is determined by the disease, not just the incision size. Ask about clearance, lymph nodes and reasons an open approach might be needed.

### Should I avoid eating to rest the bowel?

Do not restrict intake independently. The team must consider nutrition, bowel symptoms and any obstruction when advising on food.

### Will bowel habits return immediately to normal?

Not necessarily. The removed segment, stoma and other treatments influence bowel function. Ask for guidance specific to the operation performed.

### Why are more appointments needed after the tumour is removed?

Pathology, recovery and possible additional treatment still need review. Removal of the visible tumour is one part of a wider cancer-care plan.

## Prepare for the whole pathway

A colorectal cancer surgery checklist should connect staging, the operation, recovery and the next oncology decision. Agree on bowel and medicine instructions, discuss a possible stoma early and protect nutrition without delaying treatment yourself. Leave with a clear plan for urgent symptoms and final pathology review.
