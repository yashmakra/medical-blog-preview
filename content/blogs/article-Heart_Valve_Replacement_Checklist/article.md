# Heart Valve Replacement Checklist: Cardiac Clearances & Surgery Prep

A valve operation is planned around a particular problem: a valve that has become too narrow, one that leaks, or a combination affecting how blood moves through the heart. Breathlessness, reduced exercise capacity or an abnormal scan may bring you to a cardiologist, but the decision to replace a valve requires more than a single test result. The valve involved, symptoms, heart function, other illnesses and your priorities all influence the recommendation.

This heart valve replacement checklist explains how to prepare for that decision and the admission that may follow. It covers imaging, dental and infection questions, replacement-valve choices, blood-thinner planning and the support needed after discharge. It also distinguishes an operation through the chest from catheter-based treatment; they do not have identical preparation or recovery pathways.

For patients in India, practical preparation includes checking the treatment location, anticipated ICU care, insurance authorisation and access to later anticoagulation testing when relevant. Ask for a written plan that you and your caregiver understand. An online checklist cannot decide whether replacement, repair or continued monitoring is appropriate for your heart.

## Establish what the valve team is treating

Stenosis means a narrowed valve opening; regurgitation means blood leaks backwards through a valve. Neither term alone establishes the severity or the need for intervention. Your specialist should explain which valve is affected, what the echocardiogram shows and whether symptoms are likely to be caused by that abnormality.

Sometimes a valve can be repaired rather than replaced. In other situations, replacement is preferred because the valve cannot reliably be repaired or the underlying disease makes another approach more appropriate. Ask why the proposed procedure suits your anatomy and what could happen if treatment is delayed. Do not interpret an absence of chest pain as proof that important valve disease is harmless.

The [2025 ESC/EACTS guideline](https://www.escardio.org/Guidelines/Clinical-Practice-Guidelines/all-esc-practice-guidelines/valvular-heart-disease/) emphasises patient-centred Heart Team decisions and specialist valve centres for complex disease. That does not mean every patient needs every available scan. It means the decision should bring together relevant imaging, clinical assessment and procedural expertise.

### Understand the proposed route

Surgical replacement generally uses general anaesthesia and an operation through a chest incision, sometimes using a smaller-access approach. A transcatheter aortic valve procedure delivers a valve through a catheter in selected patients. Suitability depends on anatomy, overall risk and longer-term treatment planning. A technique advertised as minimally invasive is not automatically the safest choice for everyone.

If more than one valve, coronary artery disease or an enlarged aorta is involved, ask whether additional treatment is planned during the same admission. The operation name on the estimate should match the clinical plan discussed in consent.

## Choose a replacement with its future care in mind

Mechanical and biological valves have different long-term implications. Mechanical valves generally require lifelong anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist and regular monitoring. Tissue valves may deteriorate and eventually need another intervention. Some patients with tissue valves also need anticoagulation for another reason, such as atrial fibrillation. The [NHS explanation of valve replacement](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/heart-valve-replacement/how-its-done/) describes the broad options; your team must individualise the choice.

Discuss future pregnancy, bleeding history, access to an INR-testing service, ability to attend monitoring, lifestyle and the possibility of another procedure. Do not select a valve solely from a friend's experience or its quoted price. Ask how your preferences were considered and what follow-up each option entails.

If you travel or live far from the operating centre, identify a local clinician who can coordinate longer-term care. A safe discharge plan includes a way to obtain medicines and tests after the initial hospital package ends.

## Preoperative assessment: tests answer different questions

Bring previous echocardiograms and reports, angiography or CT images if already performed, admission summaries and a complete medicine list. The team can then judge what remains necessary instead of repeating investigations without a reason.

| Investigation or assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Transthoracic echocardiogram | Assesses valve severity, chambers and pumping function | Central to valve assessment; timing depends on existing results |
| Transoesophageal echo or cardiac CT | Clarifies anatomy or plans a particular intervention | When routine echo leaves a relevant question unanswered |
| Coronary assessment | Checks coronary disease relevant to the treatment plan | According to symptoms, risk and planned procedure |
| Blood count and kidney function | Helps identify anaemia and plan medicines or contrast use | Selected as part of major cardiac surgery assessment |
| ECG and clinical examination | Documents rhythm and current cardiovascular findings | As directed by the cardiac team |
| Dental or infection assessment | Identifies problems that could affect prosthetic-valve care | According to findings and local valve pathway |

Ask which results have been reviewed, not just which samples were collected. A new fever, infected wound, painful tooth or recent antibiotic course should be reported. Dental treatment should be coordinated with the cardiac team; do not postpone an urgent heart procedure independently to complete elective dental work.

## Medicines, anaesthesia and fasting need one coordinated plan

The team must know about anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, diabetes treatments, diuretics, blood-pressure medicines, supplements and traditional remedies. Include medicine strength, schedule and the condition it treats. If different clinicians give conflicting instructions, ask the cardiac unit to reconcile them before admission.

Stopping a blood thinner can cause clots; continuing it at the wrong time can increase bleeding. Whether interruption, alternative treatment or bridging is required depends on the drug and reason it was prescribed. Never implement a generic stop date from the internet. Ask who will tell you when treatment restarts and who will supervise monitoring after discharge.

The anaesthesiologist will review breathing problems, sleep apnoea, previous anaesthesia difficulties, allergies, kidney disease and other conditions that affect monitoring or recovery. Bring a prescribed CPAP device if the hospital requests it. Discuss pain relief, nausea prevention and how the team communicates with relatives during the operation.

Your fasting instructions should state what may be eaten or drunk and the relevant cut-off times. Diabetes and medicines that affect stomach emptying may require additional assessment. Do not prolong fasting or take carbohydrate drinks without approval. Tell staff promptly if instructions were not followed, including the amount and timing of anything consumed.

## Prepare for monitored recovery and rehabilitation

Ask what the first postoperative setting is likely to be: ICU, a cardiac recovery unit or another monitored area. After surgical replacement, temporary tubes, drains or pacing wires may be used. These are discussed as possibilities, not proof that every patient will need the same devices.

Before admission, describe your normal walking ability and the tasks you manage at home. Arrange help with travel, cooking and heavier household work. If your home has stairs or you care for another dependent person, raise this early so rehabilitation planning reflects your circumstances.

Smoking cessation support, attention to nutrition and treatment of clinically relevant anaemia can be part of preparation. Ask what activity is safe while waiting, particularly if symptoms occur with exertion. Do not begin an intensive exercise programme without the valve team's advice.

## Checklist for your admission

### Before the operation date

- [ ] Obtain the exact operation name and understand whether other procedures are planned.
- [ ] Discuss valve type, alternatives and the expected anticoagulation pathway.
- [ ] Confirm that imaging and requested blood results have been reviewed.
- [ ] Report dental symptoms, fever, recent infection or worsening breathlessness.
- [ ] Receive written medicine and fasting instructions.
- [ ] Arrange a caregiver and a workable follow-up location.

### The day before and admission day

- [ ] Confirm the campus, reporting time and financial authorisation.
- [ ] Carry identification, prescriptions, imaging access details and insurance documents.
- [ ] Follow only the team's approved eating, drinking and medicine plan.
- [ ] Tell staff about new symptoms and any missed or extra medicine doses.
- [ ] Make sure the person receiving updates has the correct contact details.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Understand wound care, activity advice and cardiac rehabilitation arrangements.
- [ ] Obtain a medicine list showing changes from your previous prescription.
- [ ] If relevant, confirm INR targets, testing arrangements and whom to contact about results.
- [ ] Know the follow-up date and urgent contact route.

## What to bring and what to ask about costs

Pack comfortable front-opening clothes, glasses, hearing aids and personal essentials that the hospital permits. Carry device cards from previous implants and a list of allergies. Leave valuables at home. Ask whether your caregiver needs separate accommodation or visiting permissions during monitored care.

Request an itemised estimate covering the valve or device, operating charges, anaesthesia, ICU, ward stay, medicines, investigations and rehabilitation. Clarify how extra procedures or a longer stay would be charged. Insurance approval is a financial process; it does not replace an individual discussion of benefit, risk or alternatives.

## Recovery: know your plan and your warning signs

The team will assess breathing, circulation, pain, mobility and the replacement valve before discharge. Recovery after a catheter procedure can differ from recovery after opening the chest. Ask for individual advice about bathing, sleep, lifting, driving, work and travel rather than adopting a fixed timetable from another patient.

Seek emergency assessment for severe breathlessness, chest pain, collapse, stroke symptoms or uncontrolled bleeding. Contact the cardiac team promptly about fever, a worsening wound, increasing swelling, recurrent palpitations or new deterioration. If you take anticoagulants, ask for specific instructions about bleeding, missed doses and urgent tests. Do not double doses or adjust treatment yourself.

Long-term follow-up remains necessary even when you feel well. Tell dentists and other clinicians that you have a prosthetic valve and ask your cardiac team about infection-prevention advice relevant to future procedures.

## Six preventable preparation mistakes

1. **Comparing valve price without comparing follow-up needs.** Monitoring and future interventions matter too.
2. **Assuming catheter treatment is suitable for every valve problem.** Ask about your precise anatomy.
3. **Stopping anticoagulation without a coordinated plan.** Clarify who is responsible for each change.
4. **Ignoring new dental or infection symptoms.** Report them before admission.
5. **Arranging discharge without local support.** Plan transport, monitoring and medicine supply.
6. **Treating a successful operation as the end of care.** Replacement valves require ongoing clinical attention.

## Questions for the valve specialist

1. Which valve is affected, and how severe is the disease?
2. Why is replacement preferable to repair or monitoring?
3. What alternatives are reasonable for my anatomy?
4. Which valve type best fits my priorities and future plans?
5. Are additional coronary or aortic procedures expected?
6. What medicine changes will you coordinate?
7. What are my main individual risks?
8. What rehabilitation and monitoring will I need locally?
9. Which symptoms should change the planned admission date?
10. Who will manage my long-term valve follow-up?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an abnormal echo always mean I need an operation?

No. Severity, symptoms, heart function and the expected course of the disease all matter. Ask how the findings affect your specific recommendation.

### Is a mechanical valve always better for a younger person?

Age is one consideration, but bleeding risk, pregnancy plans, monitoring access and preferences also influence the decision. Discuss the trade-offs rather than applying a single age rule.

### Can a tissue valve mean no blood thinners at all?

Not necessarily. Other heart conditions and the early postoperative plan can influence anticoagulation. Ask for your own medicine schedule.

### Do I need to see a dentist first?

Tell the team about dental symptoms and recent treatment. Whether assessment or treatment is needed before surgery depends on the valve pathway and clinical urgency.

### Will I definitely need an ICU stay?

Monitoring arrangements depend on the procedure and your health. Ask the hospital to explain its expected pathway and what could lead to additional support.

### Can I travel back to another city soon afterwards?

Discuss travel distance, mode, wound review, mobility and access to urgent care before booking. Fitness to travel is an individual decision.

### What if I cannot obtain regular INR testing near home?

Raise this before valve selection and discharge. The team needs to know whether the proposed monitoring plan is practical and safe for you.

### Will surgery cure every symptom?

It may improve problems caused by the valve, but other cardiac or lung conditions can also affect symptoms. Ask which changes are realistic to expect.

If more than one clinician will manage your anticoagulation, ask who receives each result and who can authorise a dose change. Record the laboratory and clinic contact arrangements in your discharge folder so a result does not sit unreviewed between services.

## Plan beyond the operation date

A useful heart valve replacement checklist connects the treatment decision with the care that follows. Confirm the valve problem, discuss repair and replacement options, settle medicine instructions and arrange practical monitoring near home. Bring unresolved questions to the Heart Team before signing consent, and leave hospital with a follow-up plan you can actually carry out.
