# Pancreatic Surgery Checklist: Whipple Procedure Pre-Op Nutrition & Tests

A planned Whipple operation involves more than the pancreas. The head of the pancreas lies beside the duodenum and bile duct, so removing a tumour in this area can require removal and reconstruction of several connected digestive structures. That is why preparation must address nutrition, jaundice, diabetes, physical function and the wider cancer-treatment plan, not just routine blood tests.

This pancreatic surgery checklist focuses on preparation for pancreaticoduodenectomy, commonly called the Whipple procedure. It explains the questions to ask about resectability, imaging, pancreatic enzymes, biliary drainage and recovery support. Other pancreatic operations have different requirements; the team should confirm the exact operation and the condition it is intended to treat.

For patients in India, practical planning includes access to an experienced hepatopancreatobiliary team, appropriate critical care, dietetic support and follow-up after travelling home. Bring original scans and previous endoscopy, biopsy or stent records. Ask for a coordinated plan rather than collecting separate instructions from surgery, oncology and diabetes clinics. Do not independently postpone cancer treatment to complete a diet or exercise programme.

## Understand what is being removed and why

The Whipple procedure is used for selected conditions around the head of the pancreas, including some cancers. The operation generally removes the pancreatic head and nearby structures such as the duodenum, with reconstruction to allow food, bile and pancreatic secretions to pass through the digestive system. The precise technique depends on the disease and surgical plan.

A distal pancreatectomy treats a different part of the pancreas, and total pancreatectomy has additional implications. Ask the surgeon to write down the operation name and explain which organs are expected to be removed. A diagram can help clarify the discussion, but an educational image cannot predict the reconstruction needed for your anatomy.

The [NCI pancreatic-cancer summary](https://www.cancer.gov/types/pancreatic/hp/pancreatic-treatment-pdq) describes surgery alongside systemic treatment for appropriate patients. Surgery is not suitable for every pancreatic cancer, and an operation intended to remove a tumour is different from a procedure designed primarily to relieve obstruction or another symptom.

## Resectability is not the same as fitness for surgery

Resectability concerns whether the cancer can be removed in a clinically appropriate way, considering its relationship to major blood vessels and any spread. Fitness concerns whether you can tolerate the operation and recovery. Both assessments matter. A person may have an anatomically removable tumour but need treatment of an infection or nutritional problem before proceeding.

Ask whether the case has been discussed by a multidisciplinary team and whether treatment is planned before surgery, after surgery or both. Recommendations depend on the tumour, stage, patient health and current oncology practice. Do not treat an older guideline's treatment sequence as a universal rule for every patient today.

Sometimes further imaging or another assessment changes the initial recommendation. Ask what uncertainty remains, which test could resolve it and when the team will review the result. If a second opinion is sought, transfer the actual scan images and pathology rather than relying only on a written diagnosis.

Consent should address the possibility that findings during surgery prevent the planned removal or require a different procedure. Discuss how those decisions would be made and how relatives would be informed. This conversation is preparation for uncertainty, not a prediction that the operation will fail.

## Imaging and preoperative assessments

Keep a dated record of scans, biopsies, endoscopic ultrasound and any bile-duct stent procedures. Contrast imaging quality and the questions it answers are more useful than simply counting how many scans have been performed. Your HPB team should decide whether existing studies are adequate.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Pancreas-focused contrast CT | Reviews tumour extent and relevant vessels | In staging and operative planning |
| MRI or endoscopic ultrasound | Clarifies a question left by other investigations | According to the disease and imaging findings |
| Tissue sampling | Establishes diagnosis when needed for management | Particularly when the result affects a treatment pathway |
| Blood count, liver and kidney tests | Assesses anaemia, jaundice and organ function | As part of major surgery preparation |
| Glucose assessment, sometimes HbA1c | Reviews existing or newly recognised diabetes | When relevant to metabolic planning |
| Nutrition and functional review | Identifies poor intake, weight loss and support needs | Before major pancreatic surgery |
| Selected heart and lung assessment | Evaluates comorbidity and anaesthetic risk | According to symptoms and medical history |

The [NICE pancreatic-cancer guideline](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng85/chapter/Recommendations) sets out imaging and nutritional considerations. Not every patient needs every listed investigation. Ask which result would change the operation or the sequence of treatment.

## Address weight loss and poor digestion early

Tell the team about reduced appetite, early fullness, vomiting, weight loss and changes in stools. Greasy or difficult-to-flush stools and weight loss can suggest problems digesting food, but symptoms need assessment rather than a self-diagnosis. Pancreatic disease can affect enzyme production, while obstruction or treatment side effects may reduce intake for other reasons.

Ask for dietetic input that reflects what you can eat, your usual Indian meals, dietary preferences and finances. A plan that depends on expensive supplements without discussing affordable food is unlikely to be sustainable. Do not start restrictive diets or avoid all fat without advice, especially when already losing weight.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement may be considered before and after resection. If prescribed, ask how to take it with meals and snacks, how effectiveness will be reviewed and what to do if supplies are difficult to obtain. Do not change doses or substitute products without clinical advice. Nutrition support and enzyme treatment serve different purposes and may both be relevant.

Report inability to keep food or fluids down promptly. The team may need to address obstruction, dehydration or another problem rather than simply suggesting larger meals. Ask what changes should trigger an earlier review while waiting for surgery.

## Jaundice and stents require an individual plan

Yellow skin or eyes can occur when bile drainage is obstructed. A stent may be needed in selected situations, but preoperative drainage is not automatically beneficial for every person with resectable disease. Ask why drainage is or is not recommended and how it affects timing.

Bring the stent record, including the date and any later infections or procedures. Fever, rigors, worsening jaundice, confusion or significant deterioration can indicate a serious problem and requires urgent assessment. Do not wait for the next routine surgical appointment if these symptoms develop.

If antibiotics have been prescribed, tell the operating team the drug and treatment dates. Do not start leftover antibiotics or assume that a functioning stent removes the need to report new symptoms.

## Diabetes, medicines and anaesthetic preparation

Pancreatic disease and pancreatic surgery can affect glucose control. If you have diabetes, ask how it will be managed during reduced intake, fasting and recovery. Tell the team about episodes of low glucose, recent high readings and the monitoring equipment you use. Do not make insulin or tablet changes without a named plan.

List anticoagulants, antiplatelets, steroids, pain medicines, supplements and traditional remedies. The surgeon, anaesthesiologist and relevant prescribing clinicians should agree which treatments continue, change or restart. A medicine being “natural” does not remove the need to disclose it.

Whipple surgery usually requires general anaesthesia and a major-abdominal-surgery recovery pathway. Discuss previous anaesthesia difficulties, heart or lung disease, sleep apnoea and the proposed pain-relief strategy. Ask how pain control will support breathing and movement without unacceptable side effects.

Fasting, any prescribed nutritional drinks and any bowel preparation must be coordinated with the unit. A hospital-specific preparation leaflet is not permission to ignore adjustments for diabetes, stomach-emptying problems or obstruction. Obtain written instructions and report any difficulty following them.

## Prepare your body and home without setting unsafe targets

Describe your normal activity and what has changed since diagnosis. The team may advise manageable walking, breathing exercises or a tailored rehabilitation programme. Ask what is safe if you feel weak or become breathless. Avoid exhausting yourself to meet an internet step target before major surgery.

Smoking cessation and support for alcohol-related risks may be discussed. Be candid about use because withdrawal and breathing complications require planning. The purpose of assessment is to arrange safe care, not to judge a patient.

Ask what help you may need with shopping, meals, medicine administration and appointments. If the operating centre is far away, consider where you will stay during early recovery and who can accompany you. Do not book a long return journey before the team confirms it is appropriate.

## Whipple preparation checklist

### During the planning period

- [ ] Know the diagnosis, operation name and treatment aim.
- [ ] Ask how resectability and fitness have been assessed.
- [ ] Share original scans, pathology and stent records.
- [ ] Report weight loss, poor intake, stool changes and glucose problems.
- [ ] Discuss dietetic support and enzymes if indicated.
- [ ] Agree the oncology, surgery and diabetes coordination plan.

### Before admission

- [ ] Obtain written medicine, fasting and preparation instructions.
- [ ] Report fever, worsening jaundice, vomiting or new illness.
- [ ] Confirm the hospital, admission time and caregiver contact.
- [ ] Bring identification, insurance approval, prescriptions and clinical records.
- [ ] Arrange transport, practical home support and follow-up accommodation if needed.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Understand what operation was actually performed.
- [ ] Obtain a clear food, enzyme, glucose and medicine plan.
- [ ] Learn wound or drain care if relevant.
- [ ] Confirm pathology discussion and oncology review.
- [ ] Know whom to contact about fever, vomiting, pain or inability to eat.

## What to expect after surgery

Monitoring, pain relief, fluids, breathing support and mobilisation are adjusted to the operation and your condition. Some patients need a higher-dependency or intensive-care setting. Drains and tubes may be used, but not every patient has the same devices or the same removal schedule.

The return to oral food is assessed individually. Dietetic advice may include how to manage smaller meals, poor appetite or enzyme treatment. Ask for guidance if food causes symptoms instead of silently reducing intake. New diabetes or changes in existing diabetes may need continuing review.

Complications can include problems with the new digestive joins, infection, bleeding or delayed stomach emptying. These possibilities should be discussed in consent without suggesting a fixed personal risk from a general percentage. Ask the surgeon which risks are most relevant to you and how they are monitored.

After discharge, increasing abdominal pain, fever, persistent vomiting, inability to maintain fluids, wound deterioration or significant glucose problems need prompt assessment. Collapse, severe breathlessness, chest pain or major bleeding requires emergency care. Contact the team early rather than attributing every problem to a large operation.

## Plan pathology, costs and continuing treatment

The final pathology may influence staging and further treatment. Confirm the date and responsible clinician for discussing it. Ask how the HPB and oncology teams will coordinate once you are well enough for the next stage of care.

Request an itemised hospital estimate covering the operation, anaesthesia, critical care, ward stay, investigations, medicines and anticipated follow-up. Clarify how additional procedures or complications affect charges. Enzymes, glucose supplies and nutrition support after discharge may need a separate financial plan.

Keep copies of the operative summary, pathology and discharge prescription. If care continues in another city, ask which records and monitoring results the local team will need. A written handover reduces confusion about who is responsible for nutrition, diabetes and cancer treatment.

## Six mistakes to avoid

1. **Treating every pancreatic operation as a Whipple procedure.** Confirm the anatomy and operation.
2. **Confusing removable disease with fitness for surgery.** Both assessments are necessary.
3. **Restricting food while losing weight.** Ask for practical dietetic advice.
4. **Assuming jaundice always requires a stent first.** Discuss the individual indication.
5. **Changing enzymes or diabetes medicines yourself.** Obtain coordinated instructions.
6. **Leaving follow-up until after travelling home.** Plan access to the HPB team and oncology results.

## Questions for the HPB surgeon

1. What is the diagnosis, and which operation is planned?
2. What makes the disease resectable or borderline resectable?
3. Has the multidisciplinary team agreed the treatment sequence?
4. What might change the operation during surgery?
5. Do I need biliary drainage before admission?
6. Should I receive pancreatic enzymes or nutrition support?
7. How will glucose and regular medicines be managed?
8. What critical-care and recovery support is available?
9. When will pathology and further treatment be discussed?
10. Which problems should prompt urgent contact while waiting or recovering?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every pancreatic tumour need a Whipple procedure?

No. The diagnosis, tumour location, stage and fitness determine treatment. Other pancreatic operations and non-operative pathways may be appropriate.

### Can a scan guarantee that removal will be possible?

No. Imaging informs planning, but additional findings can change the recommendation. Ask what uncertainty remains and how it is handled during consent.

### Do I need chemotherapy before surgery?

That decision belongs to the multidisciplinary team and depends on your disease and current treatment options. Avoid applying one sequence to every patient.

### Should jaundice always be treated with a stent first?

Not always. Drainage has specific indications and potential complications. Ask why the team recommends it or prefers proceeding through another pathway.

### Can enzyme capsules replace eating properly?

No. They help digest food when prescribed for enzyme insufficiency. Nutrition and hydration still require a separate, workable plan.

### Will I develop diabetes after surgery?

The risk and degree of change vary. Ask how glucose will be checked and who will coordinate treatment if it changes.

### Is a bowel cleanse routine for everyone?

No. Follow the operating unit's prescribed preparation. Do not start a colonoscopy product or a prolonged liquid diet independently.

### How soon can I return home to another state?

Discuss travel, nutrition, wound review and access to urgent assessment before booking. The team needs to consider your actual recovery rather than a fixed calendar date.

### Why is pathology still needed after the tumour is removed?

It provides information about the disease and resection that may influence further treatment. Arrange a clear result discussion and obtain a copy.

If food tolerance or stools change, bring a brief record to the dietetic review, including prescribed enzymes and meal timing. This supports assessment without turning symptom tracking into an instruction to adjust doses yourself.

## Make preparation a coordinated clinical plan

A pancreatic surgery checklist is most useful when it connects the HPB operation with nutrition, glucose control, jaundice management and oncology care. Ask how the specialists will coordinate instructions, and ensure your caregiver understands the discharge plan. The operation date is one milestone in a longer treatment pathway, not the end of preparation.
