# Angioplasty Preparation Checklist: Cardiac Tests, Medications & Admission

Planning a procedure can feel complicated when appointments, medical reports and hospital paperwork arrive at different times. This **angioplasty preparation checklist** is designed for patients and caregivers in India who want a clear, practical explanation of what to check before admission. It covers medical evaluation, medicines, fasting, hospital documents and the early stages of recovery. Some steps are routine for one patient but unnecessary for another. Your own medical history and the procedure planned by your specialist determine what is actually appropriate.

Coronary angioplasty, also called percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), uses a catheter to widen a narrowed coronary artery; a stent is commonly placed to help keep it open. Procedures may be elective for symptoms or urgent during a heart attack. Emergency treatment should not be delayed to complete a routine elective checklist.

![Illustration of a coronary artery treated using angioplasty balloon and stent](images/featured.png)

## Understanding the Procedure and Your Treatment Options

A catheter usually enters through an artery at the wrist or groin. Contrast dye and X-ray imaging guide treatment of the blockage. Your cardiologist discusses whether angioplasty is appropriate, the likely access site, the need for a stent and whether treatment may involve more than one artery. Not every coronary narrowing automatically requires a stent.

## Why Preoperative Preparation Matters

Preparation gives the clinical team time to confirm that the diagnosis and proposed approach are appropriate, recognise medical conditions that affect treatment and plan safe anaesthesia. If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, asthma, kidney disease, sleep apnoea or previous complications under anaesthesia, mention this at your assessment. It helps the team decide whether additional assessment, optimisation or monitoring is necessary. It does not mean every condition requires an automatic delay. The purpose is an informed, individual decision that takes the benefits and risks into account.

## Preoperative Medical Tests and Evaluations

| Test or evaluation | What it helps assess | When it may be appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| ECG | Review rhythm and ischemic findings | As relevant to symptoms and presentation |
| Kidney function | Assess contrast-related risk | Especially with kidney disease and diabetes |
| CBC | Assess haemoglobin and platelets | When needed for planned intervention |
| Glucose tests | Guide management of diabetes | In known or suspected diabetes |
| Prior angiogram / relevant cardiac imaging | Define target lesion and strategy | For procedure planning |
| Additional clinical tests | Investigate symptoms and comorbidity | Based on cardiologist assessment |

## Medicines Before Surgery: Discuss an Individual Plan

Antiplatelet medicines such as aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor are often central to care around coronary stents. Never omit or discontinue them without explicit direction. Discuss anticoagulants, diabetes medicines, previous bleeding, kidney disease and any allergy to contrast or earlier medicines. Timing and duration of antiplatelet therapy depend on whether the event was an acute coronary syndrome and the individual bleeding risk.

## Fasting and Dietary Instructions

For planned angioplasty, fasting requirements vary by the intended sedation, procedure and hospital policy. Emergency angioplasty for a heart attack must not be delayed simply to complete an elective fasting schedule. Ask the catheterisation team exactly when to stop solids and which drinks are permitted. If you have diabetes or take medicines that affect gastric emptying, tell the team. If you accidentally eat or drink after the stated cut-off, report what and when you consumed it rather than concealing the information.

## Documents Required for Hospital Admission in India

- Government-issued photo identification accepted by the hospital, such as Aadhaar, passport or driving licence.
- Appointment confirmation, admission letter, hospital registration number and contact information.
- Reports requested by the specialist, relevant imaging discs or digital reports, and previous discharge summaries.
- Current medicine list, written allergy information and important specialist opinions.
- Health insurance card or policy details, employer or government scheme information and cashless pre-authorisation correspondence, where applicable.
- Required consent and authorisation documents as explained by hospital staff; consent must follow a proper discussion, not merely a signature.

## Detailed Procedure-Specific Preparation

An elective PCI appointment differs from emergency PCI for a heart attack. The hospital may provide fasting instructions suited to sedation, the access route and other circumstances. Tell staff about a previous contrast reaction, asthma, advanced kidney disease, or a previous access-site problem. Ask what observation, wrist or groin precautions and follow-up will involve.

Clarify whether the planned procedure is diagnostic angiography only or angiography with possible angioplasty. Ask about cost estimates, whether a stent is expected and what happens if anatomy is unsuitable for PCI. Bring previous angiograms and details of earlier stents. If severe new chest pain develops before the appointment, seek emergency care instead of waiting for a scheduled consultation.

## Complete Pre-Surgery Preparation Checklist

### Around 7 days before (or earlier if instructed)

- [ ] Confirm the procedure, date, hospital building and reporting arrangements.
- [ ] Attend the specialist and anaesthesia assessments as scheduled.
- [ ] Complete only tests or opinions requested by your care team.
- [ ] Review all medicines, allergies and previous anaesthesia problems.
- [ ] Clarify health insurance, hospital cost estimate and any payment requirements.
- [ ] Arrange transport, caregiver support and a safe place to recover.

### Around 3 days before

- [ ] Check that required reports reached the right clinician and ask about outstanding results.
- [ ] Re-read written medication instructions and flag any conflicting advice.
- [ ] Contact the hospital about cough, fever, infection, unexpected symptoms or recent treatment.
- [ ] Prepare a document folder, prescribed aids and a small hospital bag.
- [ ] Confirm who will accompany you and who can collect medicines after discharge.

### The day before

- [ ] Verify the admission time and the exact food and drink cut-offs.
- [ ] Follow only prescribed skin, bowel or other procedure-specific preparation.
- [ ] Keep phone numbers and insurance paperwork within reach.
- [ ] Avoid making an unapproved medication change or trying an unfamiliar remedy.
- [ ] Rest where possible and arrange straightforward transport the following day.

### On the day of the procedure

- [ ] Follow the hospital’s personal fasting and medicine plan.
- [ ] Carry ID, reports, prescriptions and admission paperwork.
- [ ] Tell staff about any new symptom, unplanned food intake or medicine taken.
- [ ] Confirm procedure, relevant side or site, and discuss remaining consent questions.
- [ ] Give caregiver and emergency contact details to admission staff.

## What to Bring to the Hospital

Pack a small bag with non-slip footwear, spectacles, personal toiletries, your current medicine list, phone and charger, and any equipment that the team requested. Leave valuables at home and arrange someone to accompany you after sedation or anaesthesia.

## What to Expect After Surgery

After planned angioplasty, some patients go home on the same day or the following day. Access-site bruising can occur, and the team will provide individual instructions for activity and dressing care. Adherence to antiplatelet treatment and cardiac follow-up is critical. Severe recurrent chest pain, ongoing significant bleeding, or coldness and numbness of the access limb are urgent concerns.

### Risks to understand before consent

No procedure is risk-free. Potential issues that the specialist should place in context include bleeding at catheter entry site, artery injury or contrast-related complications, allergic or kidney-related reactions in selected patients, stent thrombosis or recurrent narrowing. The actual likelihood depends on your diagnosis, health and treatment plan. Ask which symptoms may be expected during recovery, which are concerning and when to seek emergency care. If you feel new severe chest pain, serious breathlessness, fainting or another immediately dangerous symptom, seek emergency medical help rather than waiting for a routine follow-up.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Ordering every test advertised online:** Investigations should answer clinical questions and match the treating team's plan.
- **Copying another patient’s fasting schedule:** Your anaesthesia, comorbidities and timing can make the correct instructions different.
- **Stopping medicines independently:** A decision about blood thinners or diabetes treatment may require coordination among specialists.
- **Forgetting to mention symptoms:** Recent fever, infection, chest symptoms or medication changes can affect medical planning.
- **Leaving insurance checks until admission:** A policy card does not guarantee a particular cashless approval or benefit.
- **Planning only for the day of surgery:** Transport, follow-up, wound care and home support are also part of preparation.

## Questions to Ask Your Specialist

1. What diagnosis or findings make this procedure suitable for me?
2. What non-surgical or alternative treatments should I consider?
3. What are the most important risks in my individual case?
4. Which tests and medical clearances actually need completing?
5. What kind of anaesthesia, fasting and medication plan will I follow?
6. Why is PCI appropriate for this blockage?
7. Is wrist or groin access planned?
8. What is the plan for antiplatelet medicines?
9. What if the angiogram shows more complex disease?
10. How and when will I receive stent and follow-up records?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Is angioplasty always done under general anaesthesia?

No. Many procedures use local anaesthesia with or without sedation; your team will explain the plan.

### 2. Should I stop blood thinners?

Never make changes yourself; anticoagulants and antiplatelets have different roles and require specialist planning.

### 3. Does everyone need a stent?

No. The decision depends on artery findings, symptoms and treatment goals.

### 4. What if I have kidney disease?

Tell the cardiologist so contrast exposure and kidney protection can be assessed.

### 5. Can I go home the same day?

Sometimes after planned PCI, provided the team judges observation and recovery adequate.

### 6. Which tests are required for angioplasty preparation checklist?

Tests are selected after examining your medical history, procedure and anaesthesia needs. Not everyone needs an identical panel.

### 7. Can I take my usual medicines on the morning of surgery?

Only according to your written plan. Different treatments require different instructions, and you should not independently stop prescribed medicines.

### 8. Can I drink water before the procedure?

Some anaesthesia plans permit clear liquids until a specified time, but the correct cut-off is set by your own clinical team.

### 9. What records should I take to hospital?

Carry accepted ID, booking information, relevant tests and images, prescriptions, allergy details and insurance or payment records.

### 10. What should I do if I develop fever just before admission?

Contact the treating team promptly. They will decide if new assessment or a change in timing is required.

## Conclusion

A reliable **angioplasty preparation checklist** begins with a proper specialist discussion, not an internet list of mandatory investigations. Understand why the procedure is recommended, complete the assessments you have actually been asked to undertake and obtain personalised guidance for medicines and fasting. Organise admission records, costs and support at home well before the appointment. After treatment, use the written discharge plan and seek advice promptly if a warning sign appears. This approach can make preparation more organised while leaving medical decisions with the clinicians who know your health.
