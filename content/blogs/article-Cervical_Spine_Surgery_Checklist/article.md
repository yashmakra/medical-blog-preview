# Cervical Spine Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Imaging & Neck Surgery Preparation

Neck surgery is planned around the structure causing symptoms, not the intensity of neck pain alone. A compressed nerve root can cause pain or weakness in an arm, while compression of the spinal cord can affect hand coordination, walking and balance. These problems need different discussions about urgency, goals and the possibility that some neurological changes may not fully recover.

This cervical spine surgery checklist explains the assessment before an operation, the role of imaging and the questions to ask about decompression, fusion or other techniques. It also covers anaesthetic preparation, medicine instructions and practical arrangements while neck movement or arm function is limited. The operation may aim to prevent further deterioration as well as improve symptoms.

For patients in India, bring the actual MRI images and earlier neurological records, and confirm how postoperative review will be organised if you live far from the centre. Report worsening weakness, clumsiness, falls or bladder and bowel changes promptly. Do not wait for a routine preoperative appointment if a neurological problem is progressing.

## Clarify the diagnosis and realistic purpose of surgery

Radiculopathy refers to a problem affecting a nerve root; myelopathy refers to dysfunction associated with spinal-cord compression. They may coexist. Ask the surgeon to connect the examination findings with the imaging and explain which symptoms the proposed operation is likely to address.

Decompression creates space around the affected neural structures. Fusion joins selected vertebrae to provide stability where appropriate. Other procedures may preserve movement in selected circumstances. The choice depends on the number and location of affected levels, alignment, stability, bone health and the wider clinical picture.

The [AAOS discussion of cervical myelopathy surgery](https://www.orthoinfo.org/treatment/cervical-spondylotic-myelopathysurgical-treatment-options/) describes anterior and posterior approaches and the importance of individualisation. Surgery for spinal-cord compression is not simply a treatment for age-related changes visible on a scan.

Ask whether the main aim is symptom relief, protection from further neurological decline or both. A technically successful operation cannot guarantee that longstanding weakness, numbness or coordination problems will disappear. This distinction helps set practical recovery goals and avoids treating residual symptoms as an unexpected failure without assessment.

## Record your neurological baseline

Describe difficulties with buttons, handwriting, using keys, carrying a cup or walking on uneven ground. Mention falls and changes in walking aids. These examples help the team understand function and provide a baseline for comparison after surgery.

Tell the surgeon about changes in arm or leg strength, numbness and bladder or bowel control. Sudden or progressive neurological deterioration needs prompt clinical assessment. Severe new weakness or inability to walk should not be managed through exercise videos or neck manipulation while waiting.

Also describe shoulder, wrist or peripheral nerve problems already diagnosed. Not every arm symptom comes from the cervical spine, and more than one condition can be present. Ask which problems are expected to improve with the proposed operation and which may need separate care.

## Imaging and other preoperative assessments

| Assessment | What it contributes | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| MRI of the cervical spine | Shows the cord, nerve roots, discs and soft tissues | Central to assessment of suspected neural compression |
| X-rays, sometimes movement views | Helps assess alignment or selected stability questions | When relevant to operative planning |
| CT | Clarifies bony anatomy or another specific issue | When the surgeon needs information not provided by MRI |
| Neurological examination | Documents strength, sensation, coordination and walking | Before and after intervention |
| Bone-health assessment | Informs planning when bone quality is a concern | Particularly where fusion or instrumentation is proposed |
| Selected blood tests and medical review | Addresses comorbidity and anaesthesia needs | According to health and surgical complexity |

Bring image files as well as reports and details of prior spine surgery. The surgeon may need a current study if symptoms or the plan have changed, but every old scan does not automatically need repeating. Ask how a proposed test would influence treatment.

If an implant is expected, ask what type of stabilisation is planned and how the team checks the correct level. Price or brand alone does not establish suitability. Discuss any previous implant, known material allergy or infection history.

## Anaesthesia needs a neck-specific discussion

Tell the anaesthesiologist about restricted movement, pain with certain positions, previous difficult intubation, sleep apnoea and breathing problems. The team must plan airway management and positioning with the cervical condition in mind. Do not practise forceful neck stretches to make intubation easier.

Cervical operations commonly use general anaesthesia. Discuss monitoring, pain relief, nausea and the expected recovery setting. The approach and number of levels can affect postoperative needs, so do not assume every cervical procedure has the same observation period.

An anterior operation passes through the front of the neck and may affect swallowing or voice temporarily; other complications also need to be discussed. Ask what baseline swallowing or voice problems should be recorded. New or worsening breathing difficulty or rapidly increasing neck swelling after surgery requires emergency assessment rather than reassurance over a routine message.

If you use CPAP, tell both teams and ask how postoperative use will be handled. Bring the device only as instructed. A separate plan may be needed if the usual mask or sleeping position is difficult during early recovery.

## Medicines, smoking and fasting

Give the team an accurate list of anticoagulants, antiplatelets, diabetes medicines, steroids, pain medicines, supplements and allergies. Include the reason for each treatment and any previous clot, coronary stent or major bleeding. Never independently stop a prescribed medicine because spinal bleeding is mentioned in a consent form.

If fusion is planned, discuss smoking and nicotine use candidly and ask for cessation support. The team may also review nutrition and bone health. Do not start high-dose supplements or discontinue osteoporosis treatment without an agreed plan.

Obtain written fasting and day-of-surgery medicine instructions. Diabetes, reflux or medicines that affect stomach emptying may require specific attention. Report accidental intake rather than concealing it to avoid delay. A new fever, respiratory illness or skin infection should be discussed before admission.

## Organise your home and hospital bag

Plan how you will manage dressing, meals, bathing, stairs and travel while movement or strength is limited. A caregiver should understand the restrictions without assuming the patient needs complete bed rest. Ask whether occupational or physiotherapy advice is available for daily tasks.

A collar is prescribed for some operations, not every cervical procedure. Ask whether one is needed, how it should fit, when it may be removed and who will check skin problems. Do not buy a rigid collar or use one differently from the prescription.

Pack comfortable clothes that are easy to put on, glasses, hearing aids and necessary personal items. Carry identification, prescriptions, imaging, earlier operation records and insurance documents. Ask whether any existing walking aid should accompany you and whether the discharge journey needs special arrangements.

## Cervical operation checklist

### Before admission

- [ ] Understand the diagnosis, affected levels and purpose of surgery.
- [ ] Share current images and records of previous neurological function.
- [ ] Discuss the planned approach, decompression and any fusion or implant.
- [ ] Report worsening strength, coordination, walking or bladder symptoms.
- [ ] Obtain medicine, anaesthesia and fasting instructions.
- [ ] Arrange home support, transport and any prescribed equipment.

### On the operation day

- [ ] Confirm the procedure and consent with the treating team.
- [ ] Tell staff about changed symptoms, medicines or food intake.
- [ ] Explain existing swallowing, voice or breathing difficulties.
- [ ] Ensure your caregiver knows how updates will be communicated.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Know the wound, collar, medicine and activity plan.
- [ ] Understand which neurological symptoms need urgent assessment.
- [ ] Confirm physiotherapy, imaging and follow-up arrangements.
- [ ] Ask when driving, lifting, work and travel can be reviewed.
- [ ] Obtain the operative summary and urgent contact details.

## Recovery and signs that should not wait

The team checks limb function, pain, mobility and other findings after surgery. Some patients need additional monitoring, rehabilitation or help with swallowing. The rate of neurological improvement varies, and protecting the spinal cord from further injury may be an important treatment goal even when recovery is incomplete.

Seek emergency help for breathing difficulty, rapidly increasing neck swelling, major new weakness, loss of walking ability or new bladder or bowel dysfunction. Contact the team promptly about fever, wound discharge, increasing pain or worsening swallowing. Severe symptoms should not wait for the next routine appointment.

Follow the prescribed movement and activity plan. Avoid forceful manipulation, unapproved traction and strengthening exercises copied from another operation. If fusion is involved, ask how healing will be assessed before restrictions are changed. Return to driving depends on function, medication, neck movement and clinical advice, not merely wound closure.

## Six preparation mistakes

1. **Equating every scan abnormality with a surgical indication.** The examination and symptoms must match.
2. **Ignoring hand clumsiness or repeated falls.** These can be relevant cord symptoms.
3. **Expecting all longstanding nerve deficits to reverse.** Discuss realistic aims.
4. **Starting neck manipulation while awaiting surgery.** Obtain specialist advice first.
5. **Buying or adjusting a collar independently.** Fit and use depend on the prescription.
6. **Planning discharge without support for everyday tasks.** Practical limitations need preparation.

## Questions for the spine surgeon

1. Is my problem mainly radiculopathy, myelopathy or both?
2. How closely do my symptoms match the scan?
3. What is the risk of waiting or choosing non-operative care?
4. Why is an anterior or posterior approach recommended?
5. Is fusion necessary, and how will healing be assessed?
6. What improvement is realistic for my neurological deficits?
7. What medicine and airway considerations apply?
8. Will I need a collar or rehabilitation?
9. Which symptoms need emergency assessment?
10. How will follow-up work if I live in another city?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does neck pain alone mean surgery is needed?

No. The diagnosis, neurological findings, imaging and response to other treatment influence the decision. Degenerative changes are not an indication by themselves.

### What is the difference between a pinched nerve and myelopathy?

A nerve-root problem often affects an arm in a particular pattern. Spinal-cord dysfunction can also affect coordination, walking and other functions. A clinician must assess the distinction.

### Is fusion included in every operation?

No. Decompression, stabilisation and motion-preserving options have different indications. Ask what is proposed for your levels and alignment.

### Do I need another MRI before admission?

That depends on the age and quality of previous imaging and any clinical change. Ask why an updated study is needed.

### Will I always need a neck collar?

No. The surgeon decides based on the operation and individual circumstances. Obtain fitting and care instructions if one is prescribed.

### Can I stop pain medicines when surgery is booked?

Do not make abrupt changes without advice, especially after regular use. The team needs to plan pain treatment before and after surgery.

### Is swallowing difficulty after an anterior approach always harmless?

No. Some symptoms can occur during recovery, but severe or worsening swallowing difficulty, breathing problems or swelling needs timely assessment.

### Can an operation guarantee complete recovery of hand strength?

No. Recovery depends partly on the severity and duration of nerve or cord injury. Discuss improvement and prevention of further decline separately.

## Prepare around neurological safety and daily function

A cervical spine surgery checklist should establish the diagnosis, clarify the operation's goals and protect a coordinated anaesthetic and medicine plan. Record your baseline function, report deterioration early and organise support for recovery. Leave hospital knowing both the follow-up milestones and the symptoms that need urgent assessment.
