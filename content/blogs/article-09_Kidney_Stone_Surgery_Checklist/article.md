# Kidney Stone Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Prep for RIRS, URS & PCNL Procedures

Planning a procedure can feel complicated when appointments, medical reports and hospital paperwork arrive at different times. This **kidney stone surgery checklist** is designed for patients and caregivers in India who want a clear, practical explanation of what to check before admission. It covers medical evaluation, medicines, fasting, hospital documents and the early stages of recovery. Some steps are routine for one patient but unnecessary for another. Your own medical history and the procedure planned by your specialist determine what is actually appropriate.

Kidney stone procedures are chosen according to stone size, location, symptoms, infection, anatomy and previous treatment. URS (ureteroscopy) passes a scope through the urinary tract; RIRS uses flexible retrograde access to treat stones within the kidney; and PCNL accesses larger kidney stones through a small tract made through the back. They have different preparation and recovery requirements.

## Understanding the Procedure and Your Treatment Options

Imaging, often ultrasound or CT depending on the situation, helps describe stone burden and urinary anatomy. Urine testing is particularly important before endoscopic stone procedures, because infection can lead to serious complications. Patients with an obstructed infected kidney require urgent assessment and often drainage before definitive stone treatment, not simply an elective surgery date.

## Why Preoperative Preparation Matters

Preparation gives the clinical team time to confirm that the diagnosis and proposed approach are appropriate, recognise medical conditions that affect treatment and plan safe anaesthesia. If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, asthma, kidney disease, sleep apnoea or previous complications under anaesthesia, mention this at your assessment. It helps the team decide whether additional assessment, optimisation or monitoring is necessary. It does not mean every condition requires an automatic delay. The purpose is an informed, individual decision that takes the benefits and risks into account.

## Preoperative Medical Tests and Evaluations

| Test or evaluation | What it helps assess | When it may be appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| Ultrasound or CT imaging | Define stone location and size | For selection and planning of procedure |
| Urine analysis / urine culture | Detect infection risk | Before planned endourological intervention |
| Kidney function tests | Assess kidney health | Commonly relevant to stone surgery |
| CBC | Evaluate anaemia or infection-related concerns | Depending on procedure and clinical state |
| Coagulation assessment | Review bleeding risk | Especially before PCNL or with relevant medication |
| Additional imaging | Map collecting system or access route | For complex stone anatomy |

## Medicines Before Surgery: Discuss an Individual Plan

Explain whether you take blood thinners, anti-inflammatory medicines, diabetes treatments or immunosuppressants. PCNL and endoscopic ureteral procedures differ in bleeding risk, so the medication plan cannot be copied between them. If a urine infection is detected, the treating team will decide on culture-guided treatment and procedure timing. Do not start leftover antibiotics on your own.

## Fasting and Dietary Instructions

Fasting helps reduce the risk that stomach contents enter the lungs during anaesthesia. Some guidance for selected healthy adults undergoing elective anaesthesia uses minimum intervals such as two hours for clear liquids and six hours for a light meal; fatty meals can require longer. **These intervals are not a personal prescription.** Your anaesthesiologist may set different requirements for your health and procedure. Milk-containing tea, smoothies and solid food are not interchangeable with clear water. Confirm the exact cut-off time, particularly if your procedure time changes. [2]

## Documents Required for Hospital Admission in India

- Government-issued photo identification accepted by the hospital, such as Aadhaar, passport or driving licence.
- Appointment confirmation, admission letter, hospital registration number and contact information.
- Reports requested by the specialist, relevant imaging discs or digital reports, and previous discharge summaries.
- Current medicine list, written allergy information and important specialist opinions.
- Health insurance card or policy details, employer or government scheme information and cashless pre-authorisation correspondence, where applicable.
- Required consent and authorisation documents as explained by hospital staff; consent must follow a proper discussion, not merely a signature.

## Detailed Procedure-Specific Preparation

URS often targets ureteric stones; RIRS uses a flexible ureteroscope to reach stones inside the kidney; PCNL creates direct access for stones that are large or complex. Ask whether the surgeon expects to leave a temporary ureteral stent, nephrostomy tube or both. These are not always used, and their removal or follow-up must be clearly documented.

Notify the urology team about fever, chills, worsening flank pain, reduced urine output or vomiting. A stone plus infection can require urgent care. Request a written plan for culture results, antibiotic instructions if prescribed and follow-up imaging. When relevant, discuss analysis of passed or removed stone fragments to guide future prevention.

## Complete Pre-Surgery Preparation Checklist

### Around 7 days before (or earlier if instructed)

- [ ] Confirm the procedure, date, hospital building and reporting arrangements.
- [ ] Attend the specialist and anaesthesia assessments as scheduled.
- [ ] Complete only tests or opinions requested by your care team.
- [ ] Review all medicines, allergies and previous anaesthesia problems.
- [ ] Clarify health insurance, hospital cost estimate and any payment requirements.
- [ ] Arrange transport, caregiver support and a safe place to recover.

### Around 3 days before

- [ ] Check that required reports reached the right clinician and ask about outstanding results.
- [ ] Re-read written medication instructions and flag any conflicting advice.
- [ ] Contact the hospital about cough, fever, infection, unexpected symptoms or recent treatment.
- [ ] Prepare a document folder, prescribed aids and a small hospital bag.
- [ ] Confirm who will accompany you and who can collect medicines after discharge.

### The day before

- [ ] Verify the admission time and the exact food and drink cut-offs.
- [ ] Follow only prescribed skin, bowel or other procedure-specific preparation.
- [ ] Keep phone numbers and insurance paperwork within reach.
- [ ] Avoid making an unapproved medication change or trying an unfamiliar remedy.
- [ ] Rest where possible and arrange straightforward transport the following day.

### On the day of the procedure

- [ ] Follow the hospital’s personal fasting and medicine plan.
- [ ] Carry ID, reports, prescriptions and admission paperwork.
- [ ] Tell staff about any new symptom, unplanned food intake or medicine taken.
- [ ] Confirm procedure, relevant side or site, and discuss remaining consent questions.
- [ ] Give caregiver and emergency contact details to admission staff.

## What to Bring to the Hospital

Pack a small bag with non-slip footwear, spectacles, personal toiletries, your current medicine list, phone and charger, and any equipment that the team requested. Leave valuables at home and arrange someone to accompany you after sedation or anaesthesia.

## What to Expect After Surgery

Temporary burning, blood-tinged urine, urinary frequency and stent-related symptoms can occur after instrumentation. More extensive PCNL may require a longer stay and additional monitoring. Fever with shivering, difficulty passing urine, severe uncontrolled pain or heavy bleeding needs prompt medical attention. Confirm stent removal dates before discharge.

### Risks to understand before consent

No procedure is risk-free. Potential issues that the specialist should place in context include urinary infection or sepsis, bleeding, particularly in higher-risk procedures, residual stone fragments, ureteric injury or later narrowing. The actual likelihood depends on your diagnosis, health and treatment plan. Ask which symptoms may be expected during recovery, which are concerning and when to seek emergency care. If you feel new severe chest pain, serious breathlessness, fainting or another immediately dangerous symptom, seek emergency medical help rather than waiting for a routine follow-up.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Ordering every test advertised online:** Investigations should answer clinical questions and match the treating team's plan.
- **Copying another patient’s fasting schedule:** Your anaesthesia, comorbidities and timing can make the correct instructions different.
- **Stopping medicines independently:** A decision about blood thinners or diabetes treatment may require coordination among specialists.
- **Forgetting to mention symptoms:** Recent fever, infection, chest symptoms or medication changes can affect medical planning.
- **Leaving insurance checks until admission:** A policy card does not guarantee a particular cashless approval or benefit.
- **Planning only for the day of surgery:** Transport, follow-up, wound care and home support are also part of preparation.
- **Forgetting temporary ureteral stent follow-up:** An indwelling stent requires a documented plan for removal or exchange.

## Questions to Ask Your Specialist

1. What diagnosis or findings make this procedure suitable for me?
2. What non-surgical or alternative treatments should I consider?
3. What are the most important risks in my individual case?
4. Which tests and medical clearances actually need completing?
5. What kind of anaesthesia, fasting and medication plan will I follow?
6. Why is URS, RIRS or PCNL best for this stone?
7. Was urine infection excluded or treated?
8. Will a stent or nephrostomy be left in place?
9. When will the stent be removed?
10. Will follow-up imaging confirm stone clearance?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. What is the difference between RIRS and URS?

RIRS is flexible retrograde treatment reaching the kidney; URS broadly refers to ureteroscopic treatment.

### 2. What is PCNL?

A procedure accessing the kidney through a small back tract, often selected for larger or complex stones.

### 3. Is urine culture important?

Yes. Screening and treating urinary infection before stone removal are key safety considerations.

### 4. Will I have a stent afterward?

Possibly; it depends on the procedure, stone and intraoperative findings.

### 5. When is a stone emergency?

Fever or sepsis symptoms with urinary blockage, uncontrolled pain or inability to pass urine warrants urgent assessment.

### 6. Which tests are required for kidney stone surgery checklist?

Tests are selected after examining your medical history, procedure and anaesthesia needs. Not everyone needs an identical panel.

### 7. Can I take my usual medicines on the morning of surgery?

Only according to your written plan. Different treatments require different instructions, and you should not independently stop prescribed medicines.

### 8. Can I drink water before the procedure?

Some anaesthesia plans permit clear liquids until a specified time, but the correct cut-off is set by your own clinical team.

### 9. What records should I take to hospital?

Carry accepted ID, booking information, relevant tests and images, prescriptions, allergy details and insurance or payment records.

### 10. What should I do if I develop fever just before admission?

Contact the treating team promptly. They will decide if new assessment or a change in timing is required.

## Conclusion

A reliable **kidney stone surgery checklist** begins with a proper specialist discussion, not an internet list of mandatory investigations. Understand why the procedure is recommended, complete the assessments you have actually been asked to undertake and obtain personalised guidance for medicines and fasting. Organise admission records, costs and support at home well before the appointment. After treatment, use the written discharge plan and seek advice promptly if a warning sign appears. This approach can make preparation more organised while leaving medical decisions with the clinicians who know your health.

## Questions before stone surgery

Ask which scan defines the stone size, location and obstruction, whether urine culture is clear, and why RIRS, URS, PCNL or observation is preferred. Confirm stent or nephrostomy expectations, pain and fever warning signs, medicine instructions and when the stone or fragment analysis will be discussed. Do not delay urgent care for fever with obstruction.
