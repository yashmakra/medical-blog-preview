# Kidney Stone Surgery Checklist: Preparing for RIRS, URS and PCNL

![Urologist explaining kidneys and urinary tract anatomy before kidney stone treatment](images/featured.png)

Kidney stone procedures are not all the same. A small stone in the ureter may be managed differently from a large stone in the kidney. Your urologist may discuss **ureteroscopy (URS), retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL)** or other treatment options.

This checklist helps you prepare for a planned stone procedure and understand why infection checks, imaging and follow-up are important.

> **Medical safety note:** This is an educational planning guide for patients. Your surgeon, anaesthetist and treating team must decide your tests, medicines and fasting instructions. Never change prescribed medicines on the basis of a blog.

## What should you discuss before the procedure?

Ureteroscopy uses a small instrument passed through the urinary tract. RIRS is a flexible ureteroscopic approach to stones within the kidney. PCNL accesses the kidney through a small opening in the back and is often considered for larger or more complex stones. Your stone's size, location, anatomy, symptoms and infection status help determine the choice.

Ask whether a non-surgical option, shock-wave treatment or observation might be appropriate instead. Understanding the plan includes discussing whether all stones are likely to be cleared in one procedure.

## Preoperative tests and assessments: what might be required?

Tests are selected for the individual, not automatically required for everyone.

| Assessment | Why it may be discussed |
|---|---|
| CT scan or ultrasound | Helps define stone size, location and urinary tract anatomy; your urologist chooses the imaging needed. |
| Urine test and sometimes urine culture | Checks for infection, which may need treatment before manipulating the urinary tract. |
| Kidney function and blood tests | May inform anaesthesia planning, infection assessment and safe treatment choice. |
| Other tests or specialist review | Considered according to the approach, past stone history, medicines and other health conditions. |

## Treat infection concerns before an elective procedure

Tell the team immediately about fever, chills, painful urination or signs of a urinary infection. A urine culture may be requested. **An infected, obstructed urinary system can be an emergency**, requiring urgent drainage and medical care before definitive stone treatment. Do not treat fever with home remedies while waiting for a routine procedure.

## Understand possible stents and tubes

A temporary ureteric stent may be placed after URS or RIRS, or as part of a staged treatment plan. Ask why it may be needed, what discomfort or urinary symptoms to expect, and exactly **when and where it will be removed or exchanged**. With PCNL, additional drainage tubes may sometimes be used.

## Medicines, fasting and admission

Disclose blood thinners, diabetes medicines, allergies and previous anaesthesia issues. The team will give medication and fasting instructions suitable for the proposed procedure. Do not independently discontinue a medicine. PCNL can involve a different hospital stay and recovery pattern from uncomplicated URS.

## Prepare for follow-up and future prevention

Ask whether the stone can be analysed, whether repeat imaging is needed and whether metabolic evaluation or dietary advice is appropriate for recurrent stones. These discussions are separate from the immediate surgical checklist but help plan longer-term care.

## Admission-day essentials for patients in India

Keep your photo ID, hospital admission details, current medication and allergy list, relevant test reports and scans, and emergency contact information in a single folder. If claiming insurance, carry your policy or e-card and any pre-authorisation documents requested by the hospital. Confirm payment arrangements, room availability, reporting time and caregiver access with the admission desk. Do not assume every test, medicine or implant is included in a quoted package.

## Your printable pre-surgery checklist

Save or print this list and confirm each item with your hospital.

- [ ] I know my stone size, location and proposed treatment method.
- [ ] I understand why URS, RIRS, PCNL or another option is advised.
- [ ] I have completed requested imaging and urine/infection tests.
- [ ] I have told the team about fever, chills or urinary symptoms.
- [ ] I have discussed medicines, fasting and anaesthesia.
- [ ] I know whether a ureteric stent or drainage tube may be used.
- [ ] I have a written stent removal/exchange and follow-up plan if relevant.
- [ ] I know whom to contact for fever, severe pain or trouble passing urine.

## Five questions to ask your care team

1. What makes this approach suitable for my stone?
2. Do I have a urinary infection that must be treated first?
3. Will a stent be used and when will it come out?
4. Could another procedure be needed to clear the stone?
5. What tests or changes could reduce future stone formation?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between RIRS, URS and PCNL?

They use different routes to reach and treat stones. RIRS is flexible ureteroscopy within the kidney; URS works through the urinary tract; PCNL accesses the kidney through the back.

### Why might I need a urine culture?

An infection can change the safety and timing of stone treatment, so the urologist may check and treat it beforehand.

### Does everyone receive a stent?

No. Stent use depends on the procedure, swelling, drainage and the urologist’s assessment.

### Is fever with a blocked kidney stone serious?

Yes. Fever or chills with possible urinary obstruction requires urgent medical evaluation.

## Personalise the checklist with your treating team

This checklist is a conversation aid, not a fixed test package. At the appointment, explain your symptoms, previous operations, allergies, pregnancy possibility, sleep apnoea, smoking or alcohol use, diabetes, kidney or liver disease and any prior anaesthetic problem. Carry the actual reports and scan files when available. Ask what each proposed test is intended to decide and when a result must be available. A test that is useful for one patient may be unnecessary or unsafe for another.

## Medicines, fasting and anaesthesia safety

Give the team one complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, supplements and traditional remedies. Ask who will provide the final written plan for anticoagulants, antiplatelets, diabetes medicines, steroids and blood-pressure tablets. Never stop or restart a prescribed medicine independently. Fasting hours depend on the procedure, anaesthesia and local policy; follow the hospital's written instruction rather than a friend’s schedule. If you develop fever, cough, a new infection, vomiting or a major change in symptoms before an elective procedure, contact the treating unit.

## Admission, discharge and recovery planning

Confirm the campus, reporting time, registration documents, insurance authorisation, payment process and whether an adult escort is required. Ask what clothing, mobility aid, eye drops, wound supplies or medicines should be brought. Plan transport that accommodates the expected restrictions; do not assume you can drive yourself after sedation or anaesthesia. Before discharge, obtain the medicine schedule, wound or device advice, follow-up date and a 24-hour contact route. Ask which symptoms need urgent review rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

## Questions that prevent avoidable surprises

1. What diagnosis is established, and what remains uncertain?
2. What non-operative or less-invasive alternatives are reasonable?
3. Who will perform the procedure and who provides anaesthesia?
4. Which medicines need a timed change, and who authorises it?
5. What is included in the estimate, and what could be extra?
6. What result would make the team postpone or change the plan?
7. Where will pathology, imaging or device checks be reviewed?
8. How long should I expect restrictions on work, driving and travel?
9. Which warning signs require emergency help?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use another patient's test list?

No. Test selection depends on your diagnosis, age, health conditions, medicines and the planned anaesthesia.

### Should I stop blood thinners before surgery?

Only if the responsible clinician gives a patient-specific plan. Stopping them without advice can cause serious harm.

### What if the operation is urgent?

Follow the emergency team's instructions. A planned checklist must never delay emergency assessment or treatment.

### Can I eat or drink on the morning of surgery?

Follow the exact written fasting instruction from your hospital. It may differ by procedure and anaesthetic.

### What should family members organise?

Arrange transport, a responsible escort where required, medicines, home support and a way to contact the clinical team.

### Is a package price a complete cost?

Not necessarily. Ask whether professional fees, anaesthesia, devices, tests, medicines, extra nights and follow-up are included.

### How do I prepare for a second opinion?

Take the same reports and scans and ask the second clinician to address the same diagnosis, alternatives and urgency.

### When should I seek urgent help after discharge?

Use the hospital's emergency instructions for severe pain, breathlessness, heavy bleeding, collapse, new weakness, confusion, fever or another warning specific to your procedure.

## Individual safety checks before booking

The operation name is only one part of a safe plan. Ask which finding confirms the diagnosis, what uncertainty remains and what non-operative options are reasonable. Tell the team about diabetes, kidney or liver disease, sleep apnoea, pregnancy possibility, previous anaesthetic problems, allergies and every prescribed or over-the-counter medicine. Do not stop an anticoagulant, insulin, steroid or blood-pressure tablet on your own. The surgeon and anaesthesia team should give patient-specific timing instructions.

## Tests, documents and communication

Carry the original reports, scan images, pathology results, discharge summaries, prescriptions and insurance details. A test is useful when its result could change the decision; a fixed package is not automatically appropriate for every patient. Ask who reviews abnormal results, how long reports remain valid and whether a repeat test is needed at the operating hospital. Confirm the admission building, consent appointment, payment or authorisation requirements and the phone number for changes before travelling.

## Anaesthesia, fasting and medicines

Fasting times depend on the procedure, anaesthetic, age, reflux risk and hospital protocol. Follow the written instructions from your treating team rather than a generic internet schedule. Ask when clear fluids, diabetes medicines and morning tablets should be taken. Tell the anaesthetist about loose teeth, difficult airway history, snoring, smoking and recent infection. If you become febrile, develop a cough, have vomiting or a new medical problem, contact the hospital; do not conceal it to protect a date.

## Home and discharge plan

Arrange an adult escort when advised, transport suited to your restrictions and help with meals, bathing, stairs, children or work. Ask how pain, wound or eye care, mobility, diet, catheter or drain care and follow-up will be managed. Know which symptoms require an urgent call or emergency assessment. Do not drive, fly or return to strenuous work until the treating clinician clears you. Keep the discharge summary and medicine list accessible to family and any local doctor.

## Questions that improve consent

Ask: What benefit is realistic for me? What happens if I wait? Which alternative approach is reasonable? Who will perform and supervise the procedure? What complications are most relevant to my illnesses? What would make the team change the plan during the operation? Which costs, implants, tests or extra nights are excluded? Where will pathology or imaging be reviewed? Who should I call after discharge? A clear answer is more useful than a technology label or a guaranteed outcome claim.

## Frequently asked questions: practical answers

### Can I use a relative's preparation list?

Use it only to create questions. Your diagnosis, medicines, anaesthetic and hospital instructions may be different.

### Should I take all reports to a second opinion?

Yes. Bring reports and the actual scan or pathology files where available, plus a current medicine list.

### Can I change the date without telling the team?

No. Contact the hospital so risks, medicines, fasting and authorisation can be rechecked.

### Is a package price a complete estimate?

Not necessarily. Request itemised professional, anaesthetic, theatre, device, tests, medicines, room and follow-up costs.

### When is urgent help needed?

Follow the discharge advice. Severe breathlessness, chest pain, heavy bleeding, confusion, fainting or rapidly worsening symptoms need urgent assessment.

### Does a newer device guarantee a better result?

No. The indication, evidence, clinician experience and your risks matter more than a product name.

### Who decides which tests are necessary?

The treating clinician and anaesthesia team decide after reviewing your history and the planned procedure.

### What should a caregiver know?

They should know medicines, transport arrangements, warning signs, the hospital contact and every required review date.
