# Shoulder Replacement Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Physicals & Rehab Prep

Shoulder replacement is usually considered when joint damage causes pain and loss of function that other treatment has not adequately relieved. Preparing for it includes understanding the implant choice and planning how you will dress, wash, sleep and move around while protecting the operated arm. A list of blood tests alone cannot prepare you for those practical changes.

This shoulder replacement checklist explains the assessment before surgery, the difference between anatomical and reverse replacement and the questions to ask about rehabilitation. It also covers medicines, anaesthesia, hospital documents and support at home for patients in India. If the operation follows a fracture or another urgent problem, there may be less time for preparation and a different treatment pathway.

Tell the surgeon which activities matter most to you, from combing your hair to work or caring for someone else. Those goals help frame a realistic discussion of benefit and limitations. The final plan depends on the joint, rotator cuff, bone and general health; it should not be chosen from an implant advertisement or another patient's recovery timetable.

## Understand what will be replaced

An anatomical total shoulder replacement aims to reproduce the normal ball-and-socket arrangement. A reverse replacement changes that arrangement and relies more on the deltoid muscle to move the arm. The condition of the rotator cuff and other factors influence the choice. Replacement is not the same operation as repairing a rotator cuff tendon.

The [AAOS shoulder replacement guide](https://www.orthoinfo.org/treatment/shoulder-joint-replacement) describes several replacement options. Ask why one is recommended for you, which tissue needs repair around it and what restrictions that creates. Severe arthritis, particular fractures, cuff-related damage and failed previous surgery are different starting points.

Discuss alternatives such as symptom management, physiotherapy or other procedures where appropriate. Ask which activities are likely to improve and which may remain difficult. An implant can have complications, including infection, instability, fracture or loosening; future revision surgery may be necessary in some cases.

## Examination and imaging before surgery

Bring previous shoulder X-rays, scan images and notes from earlier operations. Explain weakness, numbness, neck symptoms, instability and the effect of pain on sleep. Tell the team about recent injections and any history of infection, including infection involving the same shoulder.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Examination and shoulder X-rays | Evaluates joint damage, movement and alignment | During replacement assessment |
| CT imaging | Adds information about bone or implant planning | Selected anatomy or operative questions |
| Ultrasound or MRI | Assesses soft tissues such as the cuff | When the finding could change the plan |
| Blood count and selected medical tests | Reviews anaemia or relevant health concerns | According to operation and health history |
| Further infection evaluation | Investigates suspected infection or prior complications | When symptoms, history or revision surgery justify it |

Not every scan or blood test is compulsory. Ask what each result will change and whether suitable recent imaging is sufficient. [NICE NG45](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng45/chapter/recommendations) supports tailoring routine elective preoperative tests to the patient and operation.

## Physical preparation without provoking the shoulder

Ask a physiotherapist which movements are suitable before surgery and which should be avoided. The aim may include maintaining comfortable mobility, learning safe techniques and preparing for postoperative exercises. Do not force a painful joint through movements or begin an online strengthening programme without checking suitability.

Tell the team if you rely on a walking stick, walker or both arms to get out of a chair. Restrictions on the operated arm may affect safe transfers and walking aids. The solution may require occupational therapy, alternative equipment or temporary assistance rather than simply being told to “use the other arm”.

Practise practical tasks under guidance: putting on a front-opening shirt, managing fasteners and positioning pillows. Arrange daily essentials at an accessible height. Ask how sling use will interact with bathing, sleep and elbow or hand movement, since instructions depend on the operation and any soft-tissue repair.

If the opposite shoulder is also painful, mention it before discharge planning. It may not safely take over every task. Discuss adaptations for toileting and washing in private with the therapist, rather than discovering an unmanageable difficulty after returning home.

Ask the therapist to show your caregiver how to assist without pulling on the operated arm. A useful discharge demonstration includes getting into bed, standing from a chair and fitting the sling, with an opportunity for you to practise and ask questions.

## Medicines and anaesthesia planning

Show the team prescriptions, blood thinners, diabetes treatment, steroids, pain medicines and supplements. Discuss how pain is currently controlled and whether any medicine causes drowsiness or constipation. The surgeon and anaesthesiologist should agree changes with the relevant prescriber; do not withhold treatment independently.

General anaesthesia, a nerve block or a combination may be discussed. Ask what the block is intended to do, how long altered sensation may last and how the arm should be protected while numb. Explain lung disease, sleep apnoea, previous anaesthesia problems and any previous nerve symptoms.

Follow the issued fasting instructions and clarify approved morning medicines. Ask what pain relief should be available as any block wears off. A plan should also explain whom to contact for unexpected symptoms rather than assuming all postoperative weakness is due to anaesthesia.

## Prepare the home and hospital arrangements

Arrange help with cooking, dressing, household tasks and transport. If you live alone, explain that early so support can be planned. Consider pets, small children and tasks involving sudden pulling on the arm. Do not plan to lift luggage with the operated side on the trip home.

Bring photo ID, prescriptions, reports, insurance authorisation, comfortable footwear and loose front-opening clothes. Confirm whether the hospital supplies the sling and what physiotherapy is included. Ask for an estimate identifying implant charges, room category, medicines and rehabilitation visits, rather than assuming every implant brand has the same package.

## Shoulder replacement checklist

### Before the operation date

- [ ] Understand the diagnosis and why replacement is recommended.
- [ ] Confirm whether anatomical, reverse or another replacement is planned.
- [ ] Discuss cuff condition, bone-related concerns and prior injections or infection.
- [ ] Agree safe preoperative movement and transfer arrangements.
- [ ] Obtain a medicine and anaesthesia plan.
- [ ] Organise help for dressing, meals and transport.

### Before admission

- [ ] Report fever, skin damage near the shoulder or another infection concern.
- [ ] Confirm the correct shoulder and agreed operation.
- [ ] Follow individual food, drink and medicine instructions.
- [ ] Bring scan images, records and admission documents.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Learn the sling and wound-care instructions.
- [ ] Receive the procedure-specific rehabilitation plan.
- [ ] Understand limits on lifting, pushing, reaching and driving.
- [ ] Confirm review dates and urgent contact details.

## Recovery is guided by the operation, not a fixed calendar

The team checks pain, wound condition, circulation, sensation and movement. A therapist may teach permitted exercises and practical tasks. Follow the restrictions for your replacement and associated tissue repair; a protocol for another shoulder operation may not apply.

Ask what changes should prompt review. Increasing redness or drainage, fever, severe worsening pain, a sudden change in shoulder shape or loss of expected function needs prompt assessment. New persistent numbness, a cold or discoloured hand, or major breathing difficulty also requires urgent help. Do not attempt to “put back” a shoulder yourself.

The [AAOS reverse replacement information](https://www.orthoinfo.org/treatment/reverse-total-shoulder-replacement/) explains why the muscle mechanics differ from anatomical replacement. This difference is one reason rehabilitation and activity advice must match the actual implant and surgery.

## Six preparation mistakes

1. **Assuming every replacement is the same.** Ask what your implant depends on for stability and movement.
2. **Ignoring reliance on walking aids.** Restricted arm loading may affect safe mobility.
3. **Starting forceful exercises before assessment.** Prepare using the therapist's appropriate programme.
4. **Failing to disclose prior injections or infection.** Their timing and significance need clinical review.
5. **Planning to manage every task one-handed without practice.** Arrange help where it is realistically needed.
6. **Using pain improvement as permission to lift.** Healing restrictions can remain despite better comfort.

## Questions for your orthopaedic surgeon

1. Which problem is replacement intended to address?
2. Why do you recommend this implant arrangement?
3. Is my rotator cuff intact, repairable or no longer functional?
4. Which scans and health problems affect planning?
5. What are my main risks and possible need for later revision?
6. How should I manage a walker or transfers after surgery?
7. Which movements must be protected and for how long?
8. Who adjusts rehabilitation if progress is slower than expected?
9. What implant and physiotherapy costs are included?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is reverse replacement only for older patients?

Age alone does not choose the operation. The diagnosis, cuff function, bone, previous surgery and goals matter. Ask why the proposed design suits your clinical findings.

### Is replacement a rotator cuff repair?

No. Joint replacement changes the joint surfaces; cuff repair reattaches tendon tissue. Some operations involve additional soft-tissue work, so clarify exactly what is planned.

### Will I need a sling all the time?

The team specifies when to wear and remove it, including for washing and exercises. Do not copy another patient's duration or assume discomfort means it can be discarded.

### Can I use my arm to push up from a chair?

Ask before discharge. Pushing or weight-bearing may stress healing tissues or the replacement. A therapist can teach alternatives and assess whether additional help is necessary.

### Should I finish dental or infection treatment first?

Report any infection or planned treatment to the surgeon. The team decides whether it affects timing; do not prescribe yourself antibiotics or conceal a problem to keep the date.

### When can I sleep on the operated side?

Follow the surgeon and therapist's instructions for your operation. Ask for comfortable supported positions and report pain that is severe or unexpectedly increasing.

### Can I drive once the sling comes off?

Sling removal alone does not establish safe driving. Pain, strength, control, medicines, restrictions and insurance requirements all need consideration with your team.

### How can I prepare if I live far from physiotherapy?

Agree a realistic rehabilitation service before surgery. Ask which sessions need face-to-face assessment and how written instructions and communication with local therapists will be organised.

## Link the implant decision to everyday recovery

Preparation works best when the diagnosis, implant choice and rehabilitation plan fit together. Explain your daily tasks and mobility aids, make the home practical and obtain written limits before discharge. Those details help protect the operation while you regain useful function.
