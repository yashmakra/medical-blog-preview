# Uterine Fibroid Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Preparation for Myomectomy

Preparing for fibroid surgery begins with agreeing what the treatment needs to achieve. Heavy bleeding, pressure symptoms and fertility concerns can lead to different decisions. A fibroid seen on ultrasound does not automatically need removal, and removing fibroids is a different operation from removing the uterus. These distinctions should be clear before you consent.

This uterine fibroid surgery checklist focuses on myomectomy: surgery that removes fibroids while retaining the uterus. It explains how size, number and location influence the route, why anaemia needs attention and which pregnancy-related questions should be raised early. It also covers medicines, admission documents and recovery support for patients receiving care in India.

Tell the gynaecologist whether preserving the uterus or planning pregnancy is a priority, even if you do not intend to conceive soon. Ask how the recommended treatment serves those goals and what uncertainty remains. An operation may improve particular symptoms without resolving every cause of pelvic pain or infertility. Use the guide to prepare a specific discussion rather than choosing a technique from a package name.

## Match treatment to symptoms and reproductive goals

Fibroids arise from uterine muscle and are usually benign. Their effect depends partly on location: a fibroid projecting into the cavity may raise different concerns from one on the outer surface. Describe bleeding, pain, urinary or bowel pressure and their effect on daily life. Include previous pregnancy history, fertility treatment and other pelvic conditions.

Not every fibroid requires surgery. Depending on symptoms and goals, options may include observation, medicines, myomectomy, hysterectomy or another procedure. The [NHS fibroid guide](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/fibroids/) explains that suitability depends on the fibroids and future childbearing plans. Ask what the chosen intervention is expected to improve.

Myomectomy retains the uterus but does not guarantee pregnancy or prevent all future fibroids. Hysterectomy removes the uterus and ends the possibility of carrying a pregnancy. Those are materially different choices. [ACOG's patient information](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/uterine-fibroids) also highlights that future fibroids and scarring can matter after myomectomy.

## Which route is being proposed?

Hysteroscopic myomectomy treats selected fibroids through the cervix using an instrument inside the uterine cavity. Laparoscopic or robot-assisted approaches use abdominal entry sites. An open abdominal operation may be recommended for particular sizes, numbers or locations. No route is automatically suitable for every patient.

Ask whether complete removal is expected in one procedure and whether another stage might be necessary. Clarify what happens if unexpected findings or bleeding require a change of approach. The consent discussion should address the possibility of transfusion and, where relevant, emergency measures to control serious bleeding.

If tissue will be divided into smaller pieces for removal, ask how and why. The [FDA morcellation information](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/surgery-devices/power-morcellation-during-gynecologic-laparoscopic-surgeries) describes the concern that unsuspected malignant tissue can be spread. Discuss personal risk assessment, alternatives and containment rather than assuming every “keyhole” operation uses the same extraction method.

## Assessment before myomectomy

Bring ultrasound reports, images if available, previous operation notes and a record of your bleeding. Note how often protection is changed, clots, bleeding between periods and symptoms such as dizziness or breathlessness. Report postmenopausal bleeding or other unusual changes promptly rather than assuming known fibroids explain everything.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Pelvic ultrasound | Maps fibroid size, number and position | Usually the initial imaging assessment |
| MRI | Adds detail for complex anatomy or planning | When it could change the treatment approach |
| Blood count and selected iron assessment | Assesses anaemia related to bleeding | Heavy bleeding, symptoms or other clinical indications |
| Assessment of the uterine lining | Investigates abnormal bleeding or other concerns | According to history and risk, not automatically for everyone |
| Pregnancy assessment with consent | Identifies pregnancy possibility relevant to care | According to timing, history and local protocol |
| Selected anaesthesia-related tests | Reviews health conditions affecting surgery | Based on the patient and planned procedure |

Ask whether a scan needs updating and whether endometriosis, adenomyosis or another cause of symptoms should be considered. Finding fibroids and deciding that they explain the symptoms are separate clinical judgements.

## Correct anaemia and review medicines

If you are anaemic, ask how the team will treat it and when progress will be reviewed. Iron treatment or medicine to reduce bleeding may be prescribed, and the operation date may need to take account of improvement. Do not choose a supplement dose or ignore worsening dizziness on the assumption that bleeding is expected.

List hormonal medicines, tranexamic acid if prescribed, blood thinners, diabetes medicines, supplements and allergies. Some patients receive treatment intended to manage bleeding or change fibroid-related preparation before surgery. Ask about the intended benefit, side effects and duration. Do not independently start, stop or change the prescription.

The anaesthesia plan depends on the route, duration and health assessment. Discuss previous reactions, clotting history, breathing problems and pain-relief preferences. Obtain individual fasting directions and clarify which medicines to take on the day. Bowel cleansing is not a routine requirement simply because surgery is gynaecological; use it only if your team prescribes it.

## Fertility and future pregnancy questions

Tell the surgeon if pregnancy is a goal before the technique is chosen. Ask whether the fibroid's position affects the cavity, how the uterine wall will be repaired and whether a fertility specialist should contribute to the decision. Other causes of infertility may still need assessment.

After myomectomy, advice about when to attempt pregnancy and how a future birth should be managed depends on the surgery performed. Request a detailed operative summary and keep it for future obstetric care. Do not treat a general waiting interval or automatic caesarean recommendation from another patient as your own plan.

## Admission and home preparation

Plan for the actual route, not only the diagnosis. An outpatient hysteroscopic procedure and an open abdominal myomectomy can have different recovery needs. Ask about likely observation, transport, lifting restrictions, childcare and time away from work without expecting a guaranteed discharge date.

Bring ID, prescriptions, scan reports, previous surgical records and insurance authorisation. Pack comfortable clothing and ask what menstrual protection is appropriate afterwards. Request an itemised estimate including the procedure, anaesthesia, instruments, pathology, possible admission and follow-up; discuss how additional treatment would be charged.

## Myomectomy preparation checklist

### Before scheduling

- [ ] Explain your symptoms and uterus-preservation or pregnancy priorities.
- [ ] Understand the number, location and relevance of the fibroids.
- [ ] Compare reasonable treatment alternatives.
- [ ] Confirm the proposed route and tissue-removal method.
- [ ] Assess and treat anaemia under the team's direction.
- [ ] Discuss possible transfusion and changes to the operative plan.

### Before admission

- [ ] Obtain written medicine, food and drink instructions.
- [ ] Clarify pregnancy testing and any timing-related arrangements.
- [ ] Report fever, new illness or worsening bleeding symptoms.
- [ ] Arrange transport and support with childcare or lifting.
- [ ] Carry scan records, prescriptions and administrative documents.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Understand expected bleeding, wound care and pain treatment.
- [ ] Know activity, sexual-activity and pregnancy-planning restrictions.
- [ ] Confirm the pathology and follow-up appointment process.

## Recovery and urgent concerns

The team should explain the expected pattern of pain and bleeding for your operation. Follow instructions about washing, dressings, vaginal products and activity. The presence of small abdominal incisions does not mean the uterus has finished healing or that lifting restrictions no longer apply.

Seek urgent assessment for heavy bleeding, faintness, fever, severe or worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, difficulty passing urine or another significant deterioration. Chest pain, breathlessness or collapse requires emergency care. If bleeding or pain is outside the pattern explained at discharge, contact the surgical unit rather than waiting for the planned review.

## Six mistakes to avoid

1. **Assuming every visible fibroid needs removal.** Ask how it relates to symptoms and goals.
2. **Keeping pregnancy plans out of the discussion.** They can influence route and treatment choices.
3. **Ignoring anaemia before a planned operation.** Ask for treatment and reassessment.
4. **Confusing myomectomy with hysterectomy.** Confirm exactly what is intended to be removed.
5. **Accepting tissue-extraction terms without explanation.** Ask about morcellation if relevant.
6. **Trying to conceive from a generic timetable.** Follow the advice based on your uterine repair.

## Questions for your gynaecological surgeon

1. Which symptoms are the fibroids likely to explain?
2. What reasonable alternatives preserve the uterus?
3. Why recommend this operative route?
4. Can all relevant fibroids be treated in one procedure?
5. What is the anaemia and bleeding-management plan?
6. How will tissue be removed and examined?
7. How could this affect fertility and future pregnancy care?
8. What findings could require a different approach?
9. Who will discuss pathology and ongoing symptoms afterwards?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does myomectomy guarantee future pregnancy?

No. Fertility depends on several factors, and surgery can also cause scarring. Discuss the fibroids' likely contribution and whether a fertility assessment is appropriate.

### Can fibroids return after removal?

New fibroids may develop in the remaining uterus. Ask how persistent or returning symptoms would be assessed rather than assuming any future bleeding means the same fibroids regrew.

### Does a large fibroid always require open surgery?

Not always. Size is one consideration alongside number, location, uterine anatomy and surgical judgement. Ask why the proposed route is appropriate and what alternatives are reasonable.

### Is MRI compulsory before myomectomy?

No. Ultrasound may provide sufficient information. Additional imaging is selected when it can answer a planning question or clarify uncertain findings.

### Should I delay if my period begins near the date?

Contact the treating unit for its instructions. Timing considerations differ by route and circumstances; do not cancel or proceed on an assumption.

### Will I automatically need a blood transfusion?

No. Risk depends on the operation and your health. Ask about anaemia correction, bleeding precautions and the consent process if transfusion becomes necessary.

### Is every fibroid removed with a power morcellator?

No. Extraction methods differ. If tissue division is proposed, ask about the method, personal risk factors, containment and alternatives before agreeing.

### When can I try for pregnancy after surgery?

Obtain individual advice based on the operative findings and repair. Keep the operation record so future obstetric clinicians can plan care with the relevant details.

## Make the goals and operative plan explicit

A useful fibroid checklist connects symptom relief with anaemia treatment, uterus-preservation choices and future pregnancy considerations. Confirm what will be removed, how it will be removed and who will explain the results. Those decisions are more useful than choosing by a technique label alone.
