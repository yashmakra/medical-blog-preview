# Corneal Transplant Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Clearance & Eye Prep

A corneal transplant replaces damaged corneal tissue, not the whole eye. The operation may be intended to improve vision, relieve pain or restore the eye's structure, depending on the disease. Preparation therefore begins by establishing which layers are affected and what vision is realistically achievable after treatment, particularly if the retina, optic nerve or other parts of the eye also have problems.

This corneal transplant checklist explains the eye assessment, donor-tissue arrangements, anaesthesia questions and practical preparation needed before admission. It also covers the eye-drop and follow-up commitments that continue long after the operation. Full-thickness and partial-thickness grafts have different healing needs; some inner-layer grafts use an air or gas bubble and require specific positioning and travel precautions.

For patients in India, ask how the hospital coordinates suitable donor tissue and how you will reach an ophthalmic emergency service after returning home. Bring previous eye-operation records, current drops and spectacles. Arrange support if your untreated eye also has poor vision. Do not change steroid, glaucoma or other prescribed drops without instructions from your ophthalmologist.

## Understand which graft is planned

The cornea is the clear front window of the eye. Scarring, keratoconus, endothelial disease or other damage can prevent it from remaining clear and properly shaped. Glasses, contact lenses or other treatments may help some patients; transplantation is considered when the clinical problem warrants it.

In a penetrating graft, the surgeon replaces the full thickness of a selected corneal area. Other operations replace only the diseased layers. For example, endothelial grafts address the inner cell layer. The [National Eye Institute guide](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/corneal-conditions/corneal-transplants) explains the broad purpose and risks of transplantation.

Ask for the procedure name in writing and why it suits your condition. The type of transplant influences sutures, positioning, recovery and later optical correction. A clear graft does not guarantee perfect unaided vision, and spectacles or contact lenses may still be needed.

## Eye assessment and selected medical tests

The corneal specialist should review the original disease, previous infections, surgeries, glaucoma and the health of the rest of the eye. Tell the team about herpes-related eye disease, contact-lens use, allergies and difficulties using drops. Bring any previous graft information.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Slit-lamp examination | Assesses cornea, inflammation and surrounding structures | During specialist evaluation |
| Corneal measurements or imaging | Helps define shape, thickness or affected layers | According to disease and graft type |
| Eye-pressure assessment | Reviews glaucoma-related risk and treatment | As part of eye care where relevant |
| Retinal or optic-nerve assessment | Helps estimate potential visual benefit | According to examination and visibility |
| General health and anaesthesia review | Plans the chosen anaesthetic safely | Based on age, health and procedure |

Not every patient needs the same systemic test panel. Ask why a blood test, ECG or further specialist assessment has been requested. Report an active eye infection, new discharge or a painful red eye before admission rather than waiting for the operation day.

## Donor tissue and consent

The treating centre should explain how donor tissue is sourced and screened and what arrangements apply to scheduling. Tissue availability can affect the date; do not assume that paying a deposit guarantees a particular graft day or visual result.

Discuss rejection, infection, pressure problems, graft failure, astigmatism and the possibility of further procedures in terms relevant to your eye. Ask how the underlying disease affects the outlook and whether a previous failed graft changes the plan. Avoid comparing success percentages from patients with different diagnoses.

Make sure consent covers the intended graft and any associated procedure, such as cataract treatment if proposed. If the plan might change during surgery, ask which alternatives have been discussed and how that would affect recovery.

## Medicines, anaesthesia and fasting

Show the team every eye-drop bottle and your tablet list, including blood thinners, diabetes medicines, supplements and allergies. A written list is useful, but the bottles can help avoid confusion between similarly named products. Ask which drops are needed before admission and who will prescribe the postoperative supply.

Corneal transplantation may use local anaesthesia with or without sedation, or general anaesthesia depending on the circumstances. Explain tremor, difficulty lying flat, breathing problems and anxiety about keeping still. The final anaesthetic plan belongs to the surgeon and anaesthesia team.

Follow the hospital's eating and drinking instructions. Do not assume that an eye operation requires no fasting because local anaesthesia is being discussed. Obtain a separate diabetes-medicine plan and tell staff about accidental intake or a changed dose.

## Prepare for drops, positioning and local follow-up

Practise putting in prescribed drops or arrange a reliable helper if dexterity or vision makes this difficult. Ask the clinic to demonstrate technique and explain spacing between products. Confirm how you will obtain medicines after the first supply runs out and who reviews pressure or side effects.

Some inner-layer grafts use an air or gas bubble. The [Oxford endothelial keratoplasty leaflet](https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/media/s25fhnba/116416keratoplasty.pdf) highlights the importance of the associated precautions. Ask exactly what positioning is required, whether a bubble remains and when flying or significant altitude changes are safe. Tell any clinician planning another anaesthetic about an intraocular bubble.

Arrange transport to early appointments. If you live far from the transplant centre, identify an eye service able to assess urgent symptoms and agree how it will communicate with the operating surgeon. Telephonic reassurance should not replace examination when a graft complication is suspected.

## Check that the medicine plan can be followed

Ask for a written schedule using the actual drop names and which eye receives each product. Explain if the print is difficult to read, if you confuse bottle colours or if you cannot squeeze a bottle reliably. A caregiver can help check understanding during the demonstration. Confirm how missed doses or an empty bottle should be handled and which clinic provides refills. If different eye specialists prescribe medicines, ask them to reconcile the list so an older glaucoma or steroid instruction is not accidentally replaced by an incomplete discharge summary.

## Corneal transplant preparation checklist

### Before admission

- [ ] Know the graft name, intended benefit and limitations.
- [ ] Bring previous eye records and all current drops.
- [ ] Discuss donor-tissue scheduling and consent.
- [ ] Report eye redness, discharge, infection or new illness.
- [ ] Obtain medicine, fasting and transport instructions.
- [ ] Arrange help with drops and follow-up travel if needed.

### Before going home

- [ ] Understand the drop schedule and how to obtain refills.
- [ ] Ask about shield use, bathing and avoiding pressure on the eye.
- [ ] Confirm any positioning, bubble and flight restrictions.
- [ ] Receive the first review date and an emergency contact route.
- [ ] Know which symptoms need same-day ophthalmic assessment.

## Healing and warning symptoms

Vision can fluctuate while the eye heals, and recovery differs markedly between graft types and diagnoses. Ask when refraction, spectacle changes or contact-lens fitting may be considered. Do not buy a new prescription simply because vision is temporarily different after surgery.

Follow the team's advice about rubbing, swimming, eye makeup, heavy activity and protection from injury. Continue prescribed drops unless the ophthalmologist changes them. Steroid treatment may require monitoring, but fear of side effects is not a reason to stop it independently.

The [NHS corneal-transplant guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/cornea-transplant/) identifies a red eye, pain, light sensitivity and blurred vision as urgent warning symptoms. Contact an eye service immediately if these occur after a graft; rejection can happen long after the initial operation. Sudden flashes, new floaters or a curtain-like shadow also need urgent eye assessment. Do not wait for a routine review or assume an old prescription will treat the problem.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Confusing a corneal graft with a whole-eye transplant.** Understand the structure being treated.
2. **Expecting instant spectacle-free vision.** Ask about realistic optical rehabilitation.
3. **Stopping steroid drops because the eye feels well.** Obtain specialist instructions first.
4. **Booking flights without checking for a bubble.** Ask for explicit clearance.
5. **Ignoring a new red or light-sensitive eye months later.** Graft complications can occur late.
6. **Going home without an urgent eye-care route.** Plan access before discharge.

## Questions for the corneal specialist

1. Which layers need replacement, and why?
2. What limits the possible vision in my eye?
3. Are there reasonable alternatives to a graft?
4. How is donor tissue coordinated and screened?
5. What anaesthesia is suitable for me?
6. Will I need an air or gas bubble and positioning?
7. How long-term is the drop and review plan?
8. Who will assess urgent symptoms near my home?
9. Could spectacles, lenses or further surgery be needed?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a corneal transplant the same as replacing the eye?

No. It replaces selected corneal tissue at the front of the eye. Other eye structures remain and may influence the visual outcome.

### Will I definitely have a full-thickness graft?

No. The affected layers and diagnosis determine whether a full- or partial-thickness procedure is appropriate.

### Can I keep wearing contact lenses before surgery?

Ask the clinic when to remove them and whether a lens-free interval is needed for measurements. Instructions vary by lens type and assessment.

### Are donor tissues checked?

The transplant centre should explain its tissue-source and screening process. Discuss any concerns during consent rather than assuming a risk-free procedure.

### Will I need someone to put drops in?

You may if vision, coordination or mobility makes independent use difficult. Arrange a demonstration and dependable help before discharge.

### Can I fly home after the operation?

Only with the team's approval. An air or gas bubble can create important flight restrictions, and early review also needs to be considered.

### Can rejection happen after the first year?

Yes. New redness, pain, light sensitivity or reduced vision needs urgent assessment even long after surgery.

### Will glasses still be needed?

Possibly. Graft clarity and optical focusing are different issues. Discuss when the eye is ready for refraction and further visual rehabilitation.

Budget for continuing drops, repeat visits and travel, not only the transplant admission. Ask which of these costs are included in insurance approval and what arrangements are available if obtaining medicines locally becomes difficult.

## Build a plan for continuing eye care

A corneal transplant checklist should protect both the operation and the months of care afterwards. Confirm the graft type, arrange drops and local emergency access, and understand bubble precautions where relevant. Prompt assessment of changing symptoms is as important as attending the planned operation.
