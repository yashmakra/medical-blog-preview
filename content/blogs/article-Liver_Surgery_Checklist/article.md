# Liver Surgery Checklist: Preoperative Tests, Clearances & Hospital Planning

Before liver surgery, the team must answer two connected questions: can the problem be treated by the proposed operation, and will enough healthy functioning liver remain afterwards? A small-looking lesion can require complex planning if it lies near major blood vessels, while underlying liver disease may change the choices even when the operation appears technically possible.

This liver surgery checklist explains the assessment behind a liver resection and the practical preparation that follows. It covers scans, liver-function evaluation, nutrition, anaesthesia discussions and arrangements for hospital admission in India. The exact pathway depends on whether surgery is for a primary liver tumour, a cancer that has spread from elsewhere, a cyst or another condition. Liver transplantation is a different pathway with its own evaluation and legal requirements.

Ask your hepatobiliary surgeon to translate the planned operation into plain language and to explain the alternatives. Bring someone you trust to the consultation if useful. The aim is to leave with a clear clinical plan, an understanding of the main uncertainties and written instructions for preparation, rather than a collection of unreviewed tests or supplements.

## Clarify the diagnosis and the intended operation

“Liver surgery” may mean removal of a small segment, a larger resection or treatment combined with another procedure. Ask which part will be removed and what the team expects surgery to achieve. If cancer is suspected, clarify whether the diagnosis is certain, whether it started in the liver and what staging information is still needed.

The liver makes substances involved in clotting, processes nutrients and medicines, and produces bile. An operation must account for both the lesion and the health of the remaining organ. A person with cirrhosis, previous liver treatment or other illness may need a different approach from someone with otherwise healthy liver tissue.

The [British Society of Gastroenterology liver-cancer guideline](https://www.bsg.org.uk/getmedia/07619716-2735-45d5-804e-9f83edfa8691/HCC-gutjnl-2023-331695-full.pdf) describes resection assessment as considering liver function, portal hypertension, the extent of surgery, the future liver remnant and overall fitness. These principles do not mean every liver lesion is cancer or that every patient needs the same tests.

### Discuss alternatives in the context of your diagnosis

Depending on the disease, the multidisciplinary team may consider observation, ablation, other local treatments, systemic cancer treatment or transplantation assessment. Ask why resection is favoured and whether treatment before surgery could change the plan. A second opinion can be helpful when the operation is extensive or the available options differ substantially.

If a biopsy is proposed, ask what information it will add and whether it is needed before treatment. Do not assume a biopsy is mandatory for every liver lesion, or that a scan alone establishes every diagnosis. The responsible specialists should explain how they reached their conclusion.

## Imaging and medical evaluation

Carry the actual scan images, not only the written report. Hospitals may need the digital series to assess the lesion's relationship to liver segments, vessels and bile ducts. Ask which format they accept before travelling from another city. Provide previous scans in date order so changes can be evaluated.

| Investigation or assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast CT or MRI | Maps the lesion and surrounding anatomy | According to the suspected diagnosis and operative plan |
| Liver blood tests and clotting assessment | Evaluates liver-related problems and preparation needs | Usually central to resection assessment; tests are individually selected |
| Blood count and blood-group testing | Reviews anaemia and blood-product planning | Depending on bleeding risk and hospital protocol |
| Kidney function | Helps with medicines, contrast decisions and perioperative care | Relevant to the planned investigations and health history |
| Disease-specific staging tests | Looks for findings that could alter treatment | When cancer is suspected or confirmed |
| Cardiorespiratory or functional assessment | Evaluates ability to tolerate major surgery | Symptoms, comorbidity or operative complexity justify it |

Tell the team about hepatitis, jaundice, abdominal fluid, previous confusion, alcohol intake and any past bleeding problem. Also discuss diabetes, kidney disease, heart or lung conditions, recent weight loss and previous anaesthesia difficulties. These are not reasons to hide symptoms out of fear that surgery will be delayed; they are information needed to choose a safer plan.

## Will the remaining liver be sufficient?

Ask how the team evaluates the future liver remnant: the part expected to remain after resection. Size alone does not describe how well diseased liver tissue will work. Your surgeon should explain whether additional assessment or a staged strategy is needed before committing to the operation.

Selected patients undergo a procedure to redirect blood flow before resection, such as portal vein embolisation, to encourage growth of the part that will remain. Others may need treatment in stages. These are specialist decisions, not steps that every patient should expect. The [Cancer Research UK resection guide](https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/liver-cancer/treatment/surgery/resection) describes these approaches in its liver-cancer context.

Ask what finding could make surgery unsafe or lead to a different treatment. Planning for that possibility helps families understand a changed recommendation without assuming that earlier care was pointless. Consent should cover the intended resection, important alternatives and the possibility of unexpected findings.

## Nutrition and daily preparation

Tell the dietitian if you have lost weight unintentionally, feel full quickly, struggle to eat or have been following a restricted diet. A patient can have poor nutrition despite appearing well nourished. Ask for practical advice using foods you can afford and prepare, including suitable vegetarian sources where relevant.

Do not start a “liver detox”, herbal remedy or high-dose vitamin supplement to prepare. Some products may affect the liver or interact with treatment. Show the team the packaging of anything you take. If you drink alcohol regularly, discuss the amount honestly and obtain a supported plan; suddenly changing heavy alcohol use without medical guidance can also be unsafe.

Maintain activity within the limits your clinicians consider appropriate and ask whether breathing exercises or physiotherapy preparation is useful. If walking is difficult, explain why so the team can adapt the plan. Preparation is not a fitness competition, and new chest pain, breathlessness or marked deterioration needs assessment.

## Medicines, anaesthesia and fasting

Bring a complete medicine list including blood thinners, diabetes medicines, hepatitis treatment, prescribed pain relief and supplements. The surgeon, anaesthesiologist and prescribing specialists should agree which treatment continues and which needs adjustment. Do not independently stop antiviral treatment, anticoagulants or steroids.

Discuss the likely anaesthetic, pain-control options and the expected level of postoperative monitoring. Major liver surgery generally requires general anaesthesia; the team may discuss additional methods of pain relief. Ask how they balance comfort, breathing, movement and medicine effects when liver function is a concern.

Follow the hospital's specific fasting and drinking instructions. If a nutritional drink or another enhanced-recovery measure is prescribed, clarify whether it is appropriate for your diabetes and operation. Do not improvise with a commercial energy drink. Explain accidental intake, vomiting or inability to follow the plan before anaesthesia begins.

## Organise the hospital stay in India

Request an estimate that separates the planned operation from possible additional expenses. Ask about the surgeon and anaesthesia fees, room category, intensive or high-dependency care, blood products, consumables, pathology and follow-up. A quotation should explain its assumptions rather than promise that complications can never increase the bill.

Check insurance authorisation for the diagnosis and procedure, not only the hospital brand. If admission involves referral from another city, identify one family contact for updates and arrange access to records after discharge. Ask whether a local clinician can coordinate routine checks while the operating centre remains responsible for specialist decisions.

### What to bring

Pack photo ID, authorisation papers, prescriptions, allergy information and dated clinical records. Include scan discs or secure access details if requested. Bring glasses, hearing aids and mobility aids you ordinarily need, with suitable storage. Leave valuables at home and ask what personal items are permitted in critical-care areas.

## Preparation checklist

### At the planning appointment

- [ ] Understand the diagnosis, purpose and extent of the proposed resection.
- [ ] Ask whether a multidisciplinary team reviewed the case.
- [ ] Confirm that imaging and liver-function results have been reviewed together.
- [ ] Discuss nutrition, alcohol use, hepatitis and any signs of liver deterioration.
- [ ] Learn which findings could change the operation or treatment sequence.

### Before admission

- [ ] Complete only the additional tests and treatments your team requests.
- [ ] Obtain written instructions for each relevant medicine.
- [ ] Arrange transport, caregiver support and recovery accommodation.
- [ ] Confirm the estimate, insurance decision and reporting location.
- [ ] Report fever, worsening jaundice, abdominal swelling or new confusion promptly.

### On the admission day

- [ ] Follow the individual fasting and medicine instructions.
- [ ] Carry the original reports and complete scan images.
- [ ] Confirm the agreed procedure and contact person for updates.
- [ ] Ask when the family will receive the next clinical update.

## After surgery: understand the monitoring plan

Early care may include close observation of circulation, breathing, fluid balance and blood results. Tubes or drains may be used according to the operation. Ask what they are for, how movement will be supported and what needs to happen before they can be removed. A drain is not, by itself, evidence of a complication.

Food, mobilisation and pain treatment are adjusted to recovery. The team may monitor for bleeding, infection, bile leakage and problems with liver function. Avoid using an uncomplicated patient's discharge timetable as a target you must meet. The [University Hospitals Birmingham liver-resection booklet](https://www.uhb.nhs.uk/media/0jdpysc2/pi-liver-unit-liver-resection.pdf) provides one institution's explanation of the operative pathway; your own centre's directions take precedence.

Before leaving, ask who will discuss the final pathology, whether further treatment may be needed and what follow-up tests are planned. Seek urgent assessment for increasing jaundice, confusion, significant bleeding, fever, worsening abdominal pain or difficulty breathing. Ask how to reach the operating team out of hours and where to attend if you are far away.

## Six avoidable preparation mistakes

1. **Sending only a scan report.** Surgeons may need the complete image series for anatomical planning.
2. **Equating a small lesion with a minor operation.** Location and liver health can matter as much as size.
3. **Concealing alcohol or supplement use.** Accurate disclosure allows a safer perioperative plan.
4. **Restricting food to “rest” the liver.** Unplanned restriction can worsen nutritional problems.
5. **Assuming the remaining liver will always compensate.** Ask how reserve has been assessed for your case.
6. **Leaving the pathology discussion unarranged.** Results may determine the next treatment decision.

## Questions for the hepatobiliary surgeon

1. What diagnosis and stage are you treating?
2. Which liver segments and other structures may need removal?
3. How have you assessed the future liver remnant?
4. Would a staged procedure or another treatment be safer?
5. Which risks are increased by my underlying liver condition?
6. What nutrition or hepatitis treatment needs attention first?
7. What level of postoperative observation is expected?
8. Who will explain pathology and any further treatment?
9. Which symptoms require urgent contact after discharge?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is liver resection the same as liver transplantation?

No. Resection removes part of your own liver; transplantation replaces a diseased liver with a donor organ. The indications, assessment, medicines and legal pathway differ substantially.

### Does the liver grow back after every operation?

Remaining liver tissue can increase in size, but that does not guarantee adequate function in every patient. The team assesses baseline disease and the amount and quality of tissue expected to remain.

### Will I definitely need a biopsy?

Not necessarily. Ask how imaging, blood tests and the suspected diagnosis guide that decision. Some cases require tissue sampling, while others can be planned without a preoperative biopsy.

### Does cirrhosis rule out all surgery?

It changes risk and treatment choices but does not permit a single answer for everyone. Liver reserve, portal hypertension, the lesion and overall health need specialist assessment.

### Why might surgery be delayed for another liver procedure?

The team may be improving nutrition, treating another problem or encouraging the future liver remnant to grow. Ask what the additional step is intended to achieve and how readiness will be reassessed.

### Can I continue herbal liver products?

Show every product to your team. “Natural” does not establish safety before major surgery, and product ingredients can be uncertain. Do not start or discontinue prescribed treatment on your own.

### When will I know whether further cancer treatment is needed?

The decision may depend on final pathology, scans and multidisciplinary review. Confirm who will communicate the result and the expected appointment process before discharge.

### Can follow-up happen near my home?

Some routine checks may be coordinated locally, but this needs agreement with the operating centre. Clarify who reviews results, who changes treatment and where urgent symptoms should be assessed.

## Leave with a connected care plan

A liver operation should be supported by a clear explanation of diagnosis, remaining liver function and the recovery pathway. Bring complete imaging, make nutrition and medicine plans explicit, and arrange the pathology discussion early. Those steps make the checklist useful for your actual treatment rather than an assumed standard operation.
