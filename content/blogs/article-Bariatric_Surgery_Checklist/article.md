# Bariatric Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Diets & Tests Before Weight Loss Surgery

Bariatric surgery changes how much you can eat and, for some procedures, how food is absorbed. Its preparation therefore extends beyond anaesthesia fitness. A patient may need to correct nutritional deficiencies, review diabetes treatment, plan meals with a dietitian and understand follow-up long after the hospital stay. These discussions should begin before a surgery date is promised.

This bariatric surgery checklist focuses on decisions that influence safety and everyday life: which operation fits your medical history, how a prescribed preoperative diet works, what happens to current medicines and how you will manage the early eating stages. It also addresses family support, work arrangements and the ongoing costs of supplements and reviews in India.

Obesity is a complex health condition, not a failure of willpower. Assessment should be respectful and should consider physical health, eating patterns, mental wellbeing and your own goals. An operation is one treatment option within a continuing care plan. The information below helps you prepare for a specialist appointment; it cannot establish eligibility or prescribe a diet, fasting schedule or medicine change for you.

## Start with suitability, not a package

A multidisciplinary assessment should consider your weight history, obesity-related illness, previous treatments and ability to participate in long-term care. BMI helps describe body size but does not answer every clinical question. Eligibility criteria vary between guidelines and populations; an Indian specialist should explain the criteria being applied to you rather than copying a foreign insurance threshold.

Bring records of previous weight-management programmes, medicines and any earlier abdominal surgery. Discuss diabetes, reflux, sleep apnoea, fatty liver disease, joint limitations and pregnancy plans. Explain binge eating, depression, alcohol use or other difficulties without fear of blame; these can change what support is needed before and after treatment.

The current [NICE obesity guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG246/chapter/medicines-and-surgery) describes a comprehensive preoperative assessment, specialist dietetic input and ongoing follow-up. It is guidance from another healthcare system, not an Indian eligibility or funding rule.

### Understand the differences between operations

Sleeve gastrectomy removes part of the stomach to form a narrower stomach. Gastric bypass creates a small stomach pouch and redirects part of the food pathway. These are different operations with different nutritional implications, complications and effects on existing problems such as reflux. Ask why a particular procedure is recommended for your situation.

Non-surgical treatment, including structured nutritional care and weight-management medicines when suitable, should also be discussed. Do not choose an operation simply because a relative had it or because a provider advertises the greatest weight loss. Ask how the team defines benefit and how it manages inadequate weight loss or later weight regain.

## Assessments that answer useful questions

| Assessment | What it helps establish | When it may be needed |
|---|---|---|
| Dietetic review and nutrient blood tests | Eating pattern and existing deficiencies | Before a plan is agreed; particular tests depend on risk |
| Glucose or HbA1c | Current diabetes control and treatment needs | Diabetes or suspected metabolic risk |
| Sleep-apnoea assessment | Whether breathing support is needed | Snoring, witnessed pauses, daytime sleepiness or known disease |
| Liver, kidney and other blood tests | Relevant organ function and anaesthesia planning | Selected from the history and proposed operation |
| Cardiac or respiratory evaluation | Fitness concerns that change management | Symptoms, existing disease or other clinical indications |
| Upper gastrointestinal assessment | Reflux or stomach-related questions | According to symptoms and the centre's clinical protocol |

The list is not a compulsory shopping list. Ask what each investigation will change and whether outside results can be used. Share any swallowing difficulty, persistent vomiting, previous stomach treatment or unexplained bleeding so the team can investigate appropriately.

## The preoperative diet is a prescribed intervention

Some programmes use a short, energy-restricted diet before surgery to reduce liver size and improve operative access. The exact food choices, duration and nutritional content are centre-specific. A [Royal Devon NHS diet leaflet](https://www.royaldevon.nhs.uk/media/wkuoij3g/2-week-pre-operative-liver-reducing-diet-rd-25-780-001.pdf) illustrates one such programme; its menu is not a prescription for every patient.

Ask your dietitian how to adapt the plan to vegetarian eating, food preferences, lactose intolerance, affordability and cooking arrangements. Explain if you work shifts or cannot refrigerate food. A plan that is impossible to follow needs adjustment rather than quiet substitution with an unreviewed internet diet.

If you take diabetes medicines, reduced food intake can alter glucose levels. Obtain a written monitoring and medicine plan before starting the diet. Report low readings, shakiness or other concerning symptoms according to that plan. Do not continue an aggressive diet while concealing repeated problems.

Fasting immediately before anaesthesia is a separate instruction from the preoperative diet. Check what you may eat or drink and when, whether supplements are permitted and which medicines to take on admission. Tell staff if the instructions have not been followed so they can assess safety.

## Medicines and anaesthesia need coordinated decisions

Provide the names, doses and timing of diabetes medicines, weight-management injections, blood thinners, blood-pressure treatment, psychiatric medicines, supplements and herbal products. Include the date of the last dose of any weekly injection. The team should decide how the anaesthetic and nutritional plan affects treatment; there is no single medicine-stopping rule appropriate to every patient.

Explain nausea, vomiting, reflux, swallowing problems or a feeling that food remains in the stomach. These symptoms may influence anaesthesia planning. Tell the anaesthesiologist about sleep apnoea, previous airway difficulties and any CPAP device you use. Ask whether to bring the machine and how it will be used in recovery.

Also ask about prevention of blood clots, pain relief, early movement and nausea control. These measures must take account of your mobility and medical history. Do not independently begin compression garments, injections or supplements because they appear in another patient's account.

## Build the first month at home before admission

The early eating stages need a dietitian's written plan. Clarify the allowed textures, how to take fluids, how to recognise inadequate intake and which supplements are prescribed. Avoid buying a large supply of products before the team confirms what you need. Expensive branded “bariatric” products are not automatically necessary or suitable.

Arrange help with shopping, cooking, childcare and transport to reviews. A family member can support the plan by understanding portion changes and the reasons for staged eating, rather than pressuring you to finish a normal plate. Discuss how religious fasting, festivals or travel will be handled while recovery is still being assessed.

Make a budget for ongoing appointments, blood tests and prescribed supplementation as well as the operation. If you live far from the centre, agree who will review blood results locally and how urgent problems will be assessed. Remote contact is useful but cannot replace examination when a serious complication is possible.

## Checklist from assessment to discharge

### Before committing to surgery

- [ ] Understand the proposed operation and realistic alternatives.
- [ ] Complete dietetic, medical and psychological assessments recommended for you.
- [ ] Discuss nutrient deficiencies and how they will be treated.
- [ ] Agree goals beyond the number on a weighing scale.
- [ ] Ask about pregnancy planning and contraception where relevant.
- [ ] Obtain a written estimate including likely follow-up costs.

### During the prescribed preparation period

- [ ] Follow the dietitian's plan and report difficulties early.
- [ ] Use the agreed glucose-monitoring and medicine instructions.
- [ ] Confirm sleep-apnoea or breathing-support arrangements.
- [ ] Organise photo ID, clinical records, insurance approval and caregiver support.

### At admission and before leaving

- [ ] Report new illness, vomiting or changes in medicines.
- [ ] Follow the individual anaesthesia fasting instructions.
- [ ] Receive a written staged-eating and supplementation plan.
- [ ] Know the warning signs and the centre's urgent contact route.
- [ ] Confirm follow-up appointments and laboratory monitoring.

## Recovery: focus on function and safety

After surgery the team monitors pain, breathing, circulation, fluid intake and mobility. The timing of food progression and discharge depends on the operation and your condition. Ask what amount of drinking is expected, when the next review occurs and how to take medicines that are difficult to swallow.

Seek urgent assessment for severe or increasing abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, inability to maintain fluids, fever, racing heartbeat, chest pain or breathlessness. A complication may need examination and tests even if the skin wounds look small. Do not assume that vomiting is simply a normal part of weight loss.

Long-term care includes nutritional surveillance, attention to mental health and review of obesity-related medicines as health changes. [NIDDK's bariatric information](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/bariatric-surgery/potential-candidates) emphasises the commitment to follow-up, dietary changes and supplementation. An apparently successful early result does not remove those needs.

## Common preparation errors

1. **Using BMI alone to decide eligibility.** Ask for a full assessment of benefit and risk.
2. **Starting a crash diet without support.** A prescribed liver-reducing plan needs monitoring, especially with diabetes.
3. **Hiding eating or mental-health difficulties.** Relevant support can make the plan more manageable.
4. **Assuming supplements are optional once weight falls.** The operation can create continuing nutritional needs.
5. **Comparing package prices without follow-up costs.** Include reviews, blood tests and prescribed medicines.
6. **Treating repeated vomiting as expected.** Contact the team rather than trying to push through it.

## Questions for the bariatric team

1. Which eligibility criteria are you using for me?
2. Why do you recommend this operation over the alternatives?
3. How do reflux, diabetes and sleep apnoea affect that recommendation?
4. Who will supervise my preoperative diet and medicine changes?
5. Which deficiencies need correction before admission?
6. What are the early and long-term complications I should understand?
7. Who provides dietetic and psychological follow-up after discharge?
8. What is the plan if I struggle with intake or regain weight?
9. Which ongoing expenses should I include in my budget?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the liver-reducing diet the same as long-term healthy eating?

No. It is a short clinical preparation used by some programmes. Long-term eating after surgery needs a different, sustainable plan that supports protein intake, hydration and nutritional health.

### Can I copy a friend's sleeve-surgery diet?

No. Their procedure, diabetes treatment, allergies and nutritional risks may differ. Ask for a plan that fits your operation and circumstances, including suitable Indian food alternatives.

### Will surgery definitely remove my need for diabetes treatment?

No guarantee can be made. Diabetes may improve, but medicines must be reviewed using clinical monitoring. Do not stop treatment because your food intake or weight has changed.

### Does sleeve surgery avoid every nutritional problem?

No. Smaller intake and other changes can still lead to deficiencies. The supplement and blood-test schedule should be explained for the exact procedure rather than inferred from its name.

### Do I need psychological support if I feel ready?

Assessment explores eating behaviours, expectations and available support, not whether you deserve treatment. Ask how counselling or specialist mental-health care can be accessed if difficulties arise.

### Can I have surgery if I use CPAP?

Sleep apnoea does not by itself answer eligibility. Inform the team, bring equipment if instructed and discuss airway management and postoperative observation with the anaesthesiologist.

### Should I plan pregnancy soon after surgery?

Discuss timing, nutrition and contraception before treatment. Rapid changes in weight and nutritional needs can affect pregnancy planning; obtain individual bariatric and obstetric advice.

### How can my family support me without policing food?

Invite a caregiver to the dietetic discussion with your permission. Shared understanding of meal preparation, warning signs and review appointments is more helpful than criticism or pressure to eat.

### Will there be a fixed date for return to work?

That depends on the operation, your recovery and job demands. Ask about lifting, commuting, fatigue, meal breaks and access to fluids, then agree a staged return if needed.

## Prepare for continuing care

A useful bariatric plan links the operation to realistic goals, supervised nutrition and reliable follow-up. Before booking, know who will manage medicines, how you will eat during recovery and where to seek help. Those arrangements matter beyond the initial hospital admission.
