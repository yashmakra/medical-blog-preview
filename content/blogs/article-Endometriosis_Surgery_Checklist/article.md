# Endometriosis Surgery Checklist: Pre-Op Bowel Prep & Laparoscopy Planning

Preparing for endometriosis surgery means agreeing what the operation is intended to achieve: clarify a diagnosis, reduce pain, treat an ovarian endometrioma or address deeper disease affecting nearby organs. Those aims can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A person seeking symptom control may make different decisions from someone prioritising fertility, and surgery cannot promise to remove every future symptom or prevent recurrence.

This endometriosis surgery checklist focuses on the conversations and practical arrangements needed before laparoscopy. It explains the role of imaging, consent for treatment, ovarian-reserve questions and the involvement of bowel or urinary specialists when relevant. Although bowel preparation appears in the article title, it is not a universal requirement for every endometriosis laparoscopy. Use only the regimen your operating team prescribes.

For patients in India, check whether the hospital can provide the multidisciplinary care your disease may need and how follow-up will work after you return home. Bring previous operative notes, pathology and scan images where available. Your symptoms, preferences and earlier treatments should guide planning alongside examination and imaging.

## Define the purpose of laparoscopy before consent

Endometriosis involves tissue similar to the lining of the uterus outside its usual location. Symptoms may include pelvic pain, painful periods, pain during sex or difficulties with fertility, but other conditions can produce similar symptoms. The pattern and impact of symptoms deserve a careful history rather than an assumption based on a scan label.

Laparoscopy allows the surgeon to inspect the pelvis through small abdominal incisions. It may include treatment, such as excision or ablation of selected disease, but the extent should be agreed beforehand. Ask whether the appointment is for diagnosis alone, planned treatment or a staged approach if unexpectedly complex disease is found.

The [NICE endometriosis guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations) advises discussing pain and fertility priorities and considering specialist services for endometriomas or deep disease. A normal ultrasound does not rule out every form of endometriosis. Equally, not every person with suspected disease needs immediate surgery; medical treatment and other approaches may be reasonable depending on the situation.

## Map disease and discuss fertility separately

Take a symptom record describing timing, bowel or bladder symptoms and effects on work, sleep and daily activities. Previous surgical photographs, reports and histology can be particularly helpful when another operation is being considered. Tell the surgeon what improved or worsened after earlier treatment.

Specialist ultrasound or MRI may help map deep disease and plan the team needed. Imaging cannot guarantee that every lesion will be seen. Ask what information the scan adds and whether a specialist in gynaecological imaging has reviewed it.

If pregnancy is a priority, say so before ovarian surgery is planned. Ovarian endometrioma treatment can involve trade-offs between symptom control, recurrence and preserving healthy ovarian tissue. The [ESHRE guideline](https://academic.oup.com/hropen/article/2022/2/hoac009/6537540) addresses these decisions and the need to minimise ovarian damage. Fertility-preservation discussions are individual; tests cannot guarantee future pregnancy.

Ask whether a fertility specialist should contribute before an operation, especially after previous ovarian surgery or when both ovaries may be involved. Do not interpret a recommendation to discuss fertility as a requirement to undergo assisted reproduction.

## Which assessments may be relevant?

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Pelvic examination with consent | Assesses tenderness, masses and mobility | According to symptoms and suitability |
| Pelvic ultrasound | Looks for endometriomas and other relevant pathology | In investigation and preoperative planning |
| Specialist ultrasound or pelvic MRI | Maps suspected deep disease | When bowel, bladder, ureter or complex disease is suspected |
| Blood count | Assesses anaemia when bleeding or other concerns justify it | According to history and planned surgery |
| Fertility or ovarian-reserve assessment | Supports a fertility-related discussion | When results would influence treatment choices |
| Anaesthetic and comorbidity assessment | Plans safe surgery and postoperative care | According to health and procedural complexity |

Serum CA125 should not be treated as a diagnostic test for endometriosis. Ask what any blood test is intended to answer rather than buying an “endometriosis panel”. Pregnancy testing may be discussed according to circumstances and hospital policy; tell the team if pregnancy is possible.

## Bowel and urinary involvement change preparation

Pain with bowel movements or urinary symptoms does not prove deep organ involvement, but it should be discussed. The team may arrange additional assessment or include colorectal or urological specialists. Ask whether the planned hospital and operating list can support the likely extent of treatment.

If bowel surgery is a realistic possibility, consent should address the proposed options and relevant risks, including whether a temporary stoma might be needed. This is a discussion for the specialist team; it is not a prediction that everyone undergoing laparoscopy will need bowel resection.

Do not self-prescribe laxatives, enemas, oral antibiotics or a prolonged liquid diet. Bowel preparation depends on the expected operation and local protocol. If it is prescribed, obtain the exact products, timing, hydration guidance and advice for diabetes or kidney problems. Contact the team if vomiting or inability to drink prevents you from completing the plan.

The same principle applies to urinary preparation: do not start antibiotics because you have bladder symptoms without assessment. Explain fever, burning urine or a previous urinary infection so the team can decide whether testing or treatment is needed.

## Medicines, anaesthesia and pain planning

Give the team a list of pain medicines, hormonal treatments, anticoagulants, diabetes drugs, supplements and allergies. Tell them about previous opioid use or difficult pain control. A useful plan covers both what happens before surgery and what pain relief will be available afterwards.

Hormonal therapy before or after an operation may be discussed for particular goals. Do not stop contraception or hormone treatment independently, and do not assume the same plan applies when actively trying to conceive. Ask who will coordinate prescriptions if several specialists are involved.

Laparoscopic endometriosis surgery commonly uses general anaesthesia. Discuss previous nausea, breathing problems, sleep apnoea and any difficulty with previous anaesthesia. Obtain written fasting instructions; a bowel-preparation diet does not automatically replace the anaesthesiologist's eating and drinking restrictions.

Ask about constipation prevention, nausea management and whether pelvic-health support might be useful later. Persistent pelvic pain can have more than one contributor, so postoperative care may extend beyond removal of visible disease.

## Preserve an understandable record of the operation

Ask how the surgeon documents the sites of disease and whether you can receive an operative summary, photographs where appropriate and the pathology report. This can be valuable if care later moves to another city or a fertility service. Make sure the summary distinguishes what was seen from what was treated and explains any planned second-stage procedure. If an ovary, bowel or urinary structure needed treatment, ask what that means for follow-up. Keep these records with earlier reports so a future clinician can understand the full sequence rather than relying on a remembered description.

## A checklist matched to your operation

### During planning

- [ ] Agree whether the main aim is diagnosis, pain treatment, fertility-related care or organ protection.
- [ ] Share earlier operation notes, pathology and scan images.
- [ ] Discuss the limits of consent and what happens if disease is more extensive than expected.
- [ ] Ask whether a colorectal, urological or fertility specialist should be involved.
- [ ] Obtain a clear medicine, fasting and any bowel-preparation plan.

### Before admission

- [ ] Report fever, urinary symptoms, worsening illness or possible pregnancy.
- [ ] Confirm the operating hospital and the team expected to attend.
- [ ] Arrange transport and help with meals, childcare and household tasks.
- [ ] Carry identification, prescriptions, relevant records and insurance approval.
- [ ] Follow the prescribed preparation without adding detoxes or extra laxatives.

### Before discharge

- [ ] Ask what was found and what treatment was actually performed.
- [ ] Obtain instructions for wounds, activity, medicines and bowel or bladder problems.
- [ ] Confirm when pathology results and follow-up will be discussed.
- [ ] Know the urgent contact route and longer-term symptom-management plan.

## Recovery is linked to the extent of treatment

A diagnostic laparoscopy and extensive deep-disease surgery can have different recovery needs. Ask about work, lifting, sex, exercise and travel based on the operation performed. You may need to revise the arrangements made before admission if treatment was more extensive than expected.

Discuss eating, drinking and bowel function before discharge, particularly if bowel surgery occurred. Do not attribute persistent vomiting, increasing abdominal pain, fever, an inability to pass urine or a swollen painful leg to routine recovery without seeking advice. Severe breathlessness, collapse or heavy bleeding needs emergency assessment.

Keep the postoperative appointment even if pain improves. The team should explain findings, pathology, ongoing treatment options and any fertility-related next steps. Recurrence or persistent symptoms need reassessment rather than an automatic repeat operation.

## Mistakes that can undermine planning

1. **Expecting a normal scan to settle every diagnostic question.** Discuss the remaining uncertainty.
2. **Assuming all pelvic pain will disappear after surgery.** Ask about realistic goals and other contributors.
3. **Using bowel preparation without a prescription.** The operation determines whether it is needed.
4. **Leaving fertility priorities until after ovarian surgery.** Raise them before consent.
5. **Treating deep disease as a routine day procedure.** Check the appropriate team and recovery support.
6. **Discarding old operative records.** They can help avoid an incomplete picture before repeat surgery.

## Questions to take to your surgeon

1. What is the main aim of surgery in my case?
2. What alternatives remain reasonable?
3. What does imaging show, and what can it miss?
4. Is ovarian surgery planned, and how will healthy tissue be protected?
5. Should I discuss fertility before the operation?
6. Could bowel, bladder or ureter treatment be required?
7. What treatment is covered by my consent?
8. Do I need bowel preparation, and why?
9. What will the longer-term pain or hormone plan be?
10. How will I receive the findings and pathology results?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every suspected case need laparoscopy?

No. Symptoms, imaging, response to treatment and personal priorities inform the decision. Discuss why surgery is being proposed now.

### Can endometriosis be present despite normal imaging?

Yes. Imaging may not identify superficial disease. Normal findings should be interpreted alongside symptoms and specialist assessment.

### Is bowel preparation compulsory?

No. It depends on the planned operation. Only use the regimen your surgical team prescribes; avoid adding laxatives or enemas yourself.

### Will an endometrioma always need removal?

Not necessarily. Size, symptoms, imaging, previous surgery and fertility priorities influence management. Ask about both the benefits and the ovarian-tissue risks.

### Can the surgeon treat unexpected disease immediately?

Only within appropriate consent, expertise and safety arrangements. Some findings are better managed through a planned second procedure with the required specialists.

### Does surgery guarantee pregnancy?

No. Fertility depends on several factors, and the expected benefit of surgery varies. Seek an individual fertility discussion where relevant.

### Will I need hormonal treatment afterwards?

It may be discussed for symptom control or recurrence management, depending on your goals and whether pregnancy is being pursued. Do not change treatment independently.

### What if pain persists after healing?

Return for assessment. Residual or recurrent disease, pelvic-floor problems and other pain contributors may need different approaches.

If intimate examination or discussion is difficult, ask about a chaperone, privacy and an interpreter where needed. Understanding consent and being able to express fertility or pain priorities should not depend on a family member speaking for you.

## Agree on the goal and the next steps

An endometriosis surgery checklist should make consent more specific, not turn every patient into the same preparation pathway. Clarify your priorities, the likely disease extent and the limits of the operation. Leave with a plan for recovery, results and continuing care, including fertility or pain support when appropriate.
