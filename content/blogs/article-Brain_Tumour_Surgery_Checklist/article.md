# Brain Tumour Surgery Checklist: Diagnostic Imaging & Pre-Op Neuro Prep

A brain scan showing a tumour can raise immediate questions about surgery, speech, movement and whether the diagnosis is cancer. The first consultation should distinguish what the scan suggests from what is known with certainty. Some operations obtain a tissue diagnosis, some remove as much tumour as can safely be removed, and others relieve pressure or another complication. These aims are not interchangeable.

This brain tumour surgery checklist helps patients and caregivers prepare for the imaging review, neurological assessment and practical decisions before admission. It explains why the tumour's location matters, what an awake procedure might involve and how current symptoms should be recorded. It also covers medicine planning, communication needs and recovery support for families receiving care in India.

The right plan depends on the likely tumour type, its relationship to important brain structures, your general health and your priorities. Do not change steroid or antiseizure treatment because you feel better, and do not wait for a scheduled appointment if neurological symptoms worsen suddenly. Use the guide to organise questions for the neurosurgical team and obtain instructions that match your own operation.

## What is the operation intended to achieve?

Ask the surgeon to name the proposed procedure and its purpose. A biopsy removes a sample for laboratory examination. A resection removes tumour tissue, sometimes with the aim of taking out all that can be safely identified. “Debulking” means reducing tumour volume when complete removal is inappropriate or unsafe. A different operation may be needed to manage fluid build-up or pressure.

The safest extent of surgery depends on more than the tumour's size. A lesion near language, movement, vision or other critical pathways can require careful mapping and limits on removal. Leaving some tumour may be a deliberate decision to protect function, rather than an incomplete effort. Ask which risks are particularly relevant to your location and existing symptoms.

The [National Cancer Institute's adult CNS tumour information](https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq) explains that tissue diagnosis and tests identifying important functional areas can guide treatment. Brain tumours include different diseases; not every tumour is malignant and not every scan gives the final diagnosis.

### Understand alternatives and the treatment sequence

Ask whether observation, radiotherapy, medicines or another approach is reasonable, and what information is needed before choosing. Some treatment decisions depend on pathology and molecular testing after a biopsy or resection. Do not assume that surgery alone will complete treatment or that every patient will need chemotherapy.

If more than one specialist is involved, ask who coordinates the discussion. A neuro-oncology multidisciplinary meeting may include neurosurgery, radiology, pathology and oncology, with rehabilitation specialists contributing according to need. Record which decisions are already made and which must wait for results.

## Prepare a neurological baseline

Write down when symptoms started and how they affect daily life. Include headaches, seizures, weakness, changes in speech, memory, concentration, vision, balance and swallowing. Describe changes that relatives have noticed too. A dated account is more useful than saying that everything has been “normal” or “worse”.

Tell the team whether you can walk independently, manage stairs, use your phone, prepare meals and communicate comfortably. Explain handedness, work involving fine movements, language preferences and any hearing or learning difficulty. The team may use this information when planning assessments and postoperative support.

If seizures have occurred, describe what happened before, during and after them and bring any existing records. Ask the clinicians for an individual safety plan for bathing, supervision and work. Do not drive while awaiting clarification after a seizure or when the clinical team advises against it; the applicable Indian licensing and occupational requirements should be discussed with the responsible professionals.

## Imaging and tests: what each adds

Bring the complete MRI or CT image series, reports and older scans. If another hospital performed the study, confirm that the receiving centre can open the files. A screenshot of one slice cannot replace the original examination for operative planning.

| Assessment | Purpose | When it may be recommended |
|---|---|---|
| MRI with appropriate sequences, sometimes contrast | Defines tumour extent and surrounding structures | According to lesion and surgical planning needs |
| Functional imaging or tract mapping | Helps identify relevant functional areas or pathways | Selected tumours close to critical brain regions |
| Neurological, language or neuropsychological assessment | Records function and supports mapping or rehabilitation | Symptoms and planned operation justify it |
| CT or vascular imaging | Answers questions about bone, bleeding or vessels | When the neurosurgeon needs that information |
| Blood count, kidney function and coagulation tests | Supports anaesthesia, medicines and bleeding assessment | Individually selected before the planned procedure |
| Cardiac or other specialist assessment | Investigates relevant fitness concerns | Existing illness, symptoms or operative risk |

Additional endocrine or visual assessment may be appropriate for tumours involving particular areas. Ask why any test is being ordered and whether a recent result is sufficient. The [Cancer Research UK preoperative guide](https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/treatment/surgery/preparing-for-brain-surgery) describes possible assessments; it does not mean every patient needs the entire list.

### MRI practicalities

Tell the imaging unit about implants, a pacemaker, metal fragments, kidney problems, contrast reactions or pregnancy possibility. Explain claustrophobia before the appointment so the team can plan support. Do not take an unprescribed sedative to get through a scan, particularly when a later neurological assessment is needed.

Ask if repeat imaging is needed close to surgery for navigation or because symptoms have changed. Repeating a scan may have a specific operative purpose, but you should be able to understand that purpose. Keep the latest report and images clearly labelled to avoid confusing old and new studies.

## General anaesthesia or an awake craniotomy?

Most brain tumour operations use general anaesthesia. Selected patients may be awake for part of surgery so the team can test functions such as speech or movement. An awake craniotomy is a planned technique with anaesthesia and specialist support; it does not mean being left to tolerate an operation without pain control.

If awake mapping is proposed, ask what tasks you may practise, how you will communicate and what happens if you become distressed or cannot continue. Discuss your preferred language, anxiety, hearing needs and any difficulty lying still. The team should assess suitability and explain alternatives. The [American Cancer Society surgical guide](https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/treating/surgery.html) describes functional mapping and awake procedures as selected approaches rather than universal requirements.

For any anaesthetic, describe previous reactions, sleep apnoea, dental problems and relevant heart or lung disease. Ask about nausea, postoperative pain and where you are likely to be observed immediately afterwards. The plan may change with the procedure and your condition.

## Steroids, antiseizure medicines and other treatment

Bring the exact names, strengths and timing of steroids, antiseizure medicines, blood thinners, diabetes treatment and all supplements. Include recent dose changes and any missed doses. If you have vomiting or trouble swallowing, tell the team because this can interrupt important treatment.

Steroids may be prescribed to reduce swelling around a tumour; antiseizure medicines may be needed for seizure management. They are not required in exactly the same way for every patient. Ask who will issue and review the plan, including any taper. Do not abruptly stop treatment or copy another patient's dose schedule.

Blood thinners require a coordinated decision that considers both bleeding and clotting risks. Diabetes treatment may need review if steroids or altered intake affect glucose. The [UCLH craniotomy and biopsy information](https://www.uclh.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-pages/having-craniotomy-or-biopsy-suspected-brain-tumour) specifically advises against stopping medicines without the preoperative team's direction.

## Food, hair and the admission day

Follow the anaesthesiologist's fasting instructions, including what to do with regular medicines. The permitted timing of food and drinks must come from your team. If symptoms worsen while waiting or you cannot keep medicines down, contact the treating unit rather than simply continuing the routine preparation.

Ask how the scalp should be prepared. Do not shave your head or apply new hair or skin products unless instructed; the operating team decides whether a small area needs clipping. Tell staff about scalp infection, broken skin or a recent wound near the intended site.

Remove valuables according to hospital instructions and identify who may receive updates with your consent. If you need an interpreter, communication aid or assistance signing forms, arrange it before admission where possible. Consent requires an explanation you understand, not just a signature in a busy ward.

## Prepare the caregiver and the home

Discuss possible needs without assuming a particular deficit will occur. Some patients need support with walking, language, memory, swallowing or daily activities after surgery. Ask whether physiotherapy, occupational therapy or speech and language therapy will assess you and how rehabilitation continues after discharge.

Plan a place to recover with safe toilet access, reduced trip hazards and someone who can help with medicines and appointments if needed. Put a written medicine schedule and hospital contact route where the caregiver can find them. Ask for advice about stairs, bathing and being alone, particularly when seizures or balance problems are a concern.

For families travelling across India, check whether accommodation near the hospital is needed after discharge and whether an early review or scan is planned. Ask before booking air travel. Arrange secure access to records so another emergency department can see the operation details if urgent care is needed away from the treating centre.

## Checklist before neurosurgical admission

### While the treatment plan is being made

- [ ] Clarify whether the aim is diagnosis, removal, debulking or pressure relief.
- [ ] Ensure the surgeon has reviewed complete current and previous scans.
- [ ] Record neurological symptoms and everyday functional difficulties.
- [ ] Discuss the main risks to functions important to you.
- [ ] Ask whether mapping, awake testing or special monitoring is proposed.
- [ ] Identify the person coordinating pathology and later treatment decisions.

### Before the admission date

- [ ] Obtain written steroid, antiseizure and other medicine instructions.
- [ ] Confirm whether additional assessment or imaging is outstanding.
- [ ] Arrange caregiver support, transport and any interpreter needs.
- [ ] Carry ID, prescriptions, insurance papers, scan files and relevant records.
- [ ] Confirm the likely observation area and how family updates are given.

### On the day and before discharge

- [ ] Report new weakness, seizures, severe headache or worsening alertness immediately.
- [ ] Follow the individual fasting instructions and report accidental intake.
- [ ] Learn wound-care directions and who reviews stitches or clips.
- [ ] Confirm the medicine supply, taper instructions and next appointment.
- [ ] Know where to seek urgent assessment outside clinic hours.

## What happens in early recovery?

Staff may regularly check alertness, pupils, limb movement and speech, alongside pain, breathing and circulation. These repeated observations help identify changes quickly. Tell the team about new symptoms even if you are unsure whether they are significant. A caregiver can assist communication but should not be expected to diagnose a problem.

Observation may occur in a recovery area, specialist ward, high-dependency unit or intensive care, depending on the procedure and condition. Ask what monitors, drains or dressings are being used. Eating, drinking and mobilisation begin according to clinical assessment, including swallowing safety when relevant.

Pathology may take time, especially when additional testing is needed to classify the tumour. Ask who will explain the integrated result and the next treatment recommendation. An early scan and the pathology report answer different questions; neither should be interpreted from an isolated phrase without specialist context.

### Warning signs should override routine appointments

Seek emergency help for a new or prolonged seizure, sudden weakness, major speech change, rapidly increasing drowsiness, collapse or a sudden severe headache. Fever with significant illness, worsening wound swelling or drainage, repeated vomiting or new neurological symptoms also needs prompt clinical assessment. Use the team's emergency instructions rather than waiting for an online reply.

## Mistakes that can complicate preparation

1. **Assuming the scan already establishes tumour grade.** Tissue and molecular results may be needed.
2. **Equating maximum removal with the best outcome in every case.** Preserving important function can limit safe resection.
3. **Changing steroids or antiseizure treatment independently.** Dose decisions need the responsible clinician.
4. **Hiding subtle memory or speech problems.** Baseline assessment helps interpret recovery and plan support.
5. **Shaving the scalp at home.** Follow the unit's skin-preparation directions instead.
6. **Booking travel before postoperative review is planned.** Recovery and further treatment can change practical arrangements.
7. **Leaving without a named result pathway.** Know who will discuss pathology and the next decision.

## Questions to take to the neurosurgeon

1. What is known from imaging, and what remains uncertain?
2. What exactly is the aim of this operation?
3. Which functions are most at risk from the tumour and surgery?
4. How much removal is intended, and what may limit it?
5. Would awake testing or other mapping help in my case?
6. Which medicines need a written continuation or adjustment plan?
7. What observation and rehabilitation may be required afterwards?
8. Who will explain pathology, molecular testing and later treatment?
9. What restrictions apply to work, driving and travel?
10. Which symptoms require emergency assessment before or after surgery?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a brain tumour always mean cancer?

No. There are several tumour types, including non-malignant tumours. Even a non-malignant tumour can cause problems because of its location, so diagnosis and treatment planning remain important.

### Why might a biopsy be recommended instead of removal?

The team may need a diagnosis when removal would carry excessive risk or would not be the right initial treatment. Ask what the sample will establish and how the result changes care.

### Will I have to be awake during surgery?

Only selected patients need an awake approach. The decision depends on the tumour, proposed mapping and individual suitability. Ask for a detailed discussion rather than assuming this from an online description.

### Can I skip a dose because fasting has started?

Do not make that decision independently. Ask exactly which medicines to take, when and how. Important treatment may need to continue despite fasting, using the route and timing the team specifies.

### Is temporary weakness the same as permanent damage?

No, but new weakness must always be reported promptly. Its cause and likely course need clinical assessment; patients and caregivers should not try to distinguish temporary and permanent effects at home.

### Will the entire head be shaved?

Not necessarily. The operating team decides what preparation is necessary. Ask your centre and avoid shaving the scalp yourself before admission.

### Can my family interpret the pathology report first?

They can help organise records, but terminology needs context. Arrange a consultation to discuss tumour type, grade, relevant molecular findings and the treatment recommendation together.

### When can I resume work or study?

The answer depends on fatigue, concentration, seizures, neurological function and treatment still to come. Ask about a gradual return, suitable adjustments and any occupational restrictions.

### What if I live far from the neurosurgical centre?

Agree where urgent symptoms will be assessed and how records reach local clinicians. Clarify which reviews can occur remotely and which need examination or imaging at the treating centre.

## Make the next decision understandable

A good preparation plan explains the purpose of surgery, protects essential medicines and anticipates communication and recovery needs. Keep your symptom record, scan files and questions together. Before admission, know who will contact your caregiver, discuss the tissue result and guide the next stage of care.
