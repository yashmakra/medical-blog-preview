# Pacemaker Implantation Checklist: Pre-Op Testing & Patient Guidelines

A pacemaker appointment usually follows an investigation into a slow or unreliable heartbeat. You may have fainted, felt unusually tired or had an ECG showing a conduction problem. The device helps regulate selected rhythm problems, but the reasons for fitting one, the type of device and the urgency of treatment vary between patients. Preparation begins with understanding the rhythm diagnosis rather than choosing a device by brand.

This pacemaker surgery checklist explains the assessment before implantation, what to clarify about medicines and anaesthesia, and how to prepare your home and follow-up arrangements. It also covers the device card and clinic checks that become part of life afterwards. A conventional pacemaker, a leadless pacemaker and a cardiac resynchronisation device do not have identical indications or implantation methods.

In India, confirm the exact hospital and device clinic, insurance approval and how future checks will be arranged if you live elsewhere. Do not delay urgent treatment while completing an elective checklist. If you are fainting repeatedly or have severe chest pain or breathlessness, obtain urgent clinical assessment.

## Confirm the rhythm problem and the proposed device

The electrical system normally coordinates the heartbeat. A pacemaker can deliver small electrical impulses when the heart's own signals are too slow or interrupted. It is not a general treatment for every palpitation and is different from an implantable defibrillator, which treats certain dangerous fast rhythms.

Ask the cardiologist what rhythm has been documented and whether medicines or reversible conditions might contribute. The [NHLBI preparation guide](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/pacemakers/preparing) encourages discussion of the device best suited to your condition. Device selection should reflect the heart's electrical and pumping needs, not an assumption that more leads mean better care.

A conventional system usually has a generator beneath the upper-chest skin and leads guided through veins to the heart. Selected patients may receive a leadless device delivered through a vein to the heart. Some people need more specialised pacing. Ask which approach is planned and why.

## Tests before implantation

Existing reports help the electrophysiologist understand how symptoms relate to rhythm changes. Bring actual ECGs or rhythm-monitor reports, not only a written statement that your heartbeat is slow.

| Assessment | What it helps establish | When it may be useful |
|---|---|---|
| ECG or ambulatory rhythm monitoring | Type and timing of the electrical problem | According to symptoms and previous recordings |
| Echocardiogram | Heart structure and pumping function | When relevant to device choice or underlying disease |
| Blood count, electrolytes and kidney tests | Factors affecting procedural and medicine planning | Selected during pre-implant assessment |
| Infection review | Fever, skin infection or another issue affecting implantation | Especially when new symptoms are present |
| Previous device or venous-access records | Helps plan replacement or complex access | If you already have a device or relevant procedures |

The [NHS pacemaker overview](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/pacemaker-implantation/) describes blood tests and other assessments that may be requested. It does not mean a chest X-ray or every scan is compulsory for every patient.

## Medicines and anaesthesia: ask for named instructions

List blood thinners, heart medicines, diabetes drugs, supplements and allergies. Tell the team about previous bleeding, implanted devices, dialysis access and any medicine reaction. Anticoagulant decisions around pacemaker implantation require a drug-specific plan; independently stopping treatment can be dangerous.

Many conventional implants use local anaesthesia with or without sedation. Other circumstances can require a different plan. Explain if lying flat causes breathlessness, if you have sleep apnoea or if previous sedation was difficult. Ask whether you will need a responsible adult to take you home and stay with you.

Fasting depends on the anaesthetic and hospital pathway. Obtain written eating and drinking instructions and a separate plan for diabetes medicines. Do not assume that being awake means fasting is unnecessary. Tell staff about any accidental intake or missed dose.

## Arrange the practical details before admission

Ask for the device type and anticipated costs in writing. An estimate should distinguish the generator, leads if used, implantation charges, hospital stay, programming and follow-up. Check whether remote monitoring is available, whether it carries a fee and who reviews alerts.

Tell the team about your work, driving, contact sports and occupational exposure to strong electromagnetic fields. Advice about machinery and driving depends on your condition and local rules. Do not copy UK driving restrictions from a patient leaflet as if they were Indian licensing requirements.

Prepare clothing that will not rub the chest wound and arrange help with bags, overhead tasks and transport. Ask about movement restrictions rather than immobilising the shoulder unnecessarily. If a leadless system is planned, groin-site care and restrictions may differ.

## Keep the device information usable

Before discharge, ask which details appear on the identification card and whether a paper and digital copy are available. If remote monitoring is offered, confirm what equipment or phone connection it needs, who checks transmissions and whether it is an emergency service. An automated transmission should not replace calling for urgent symptoms. If another hospital will provide follow-up, ask the implanting clinic to organise a handover that includes the device model, leads, settings and relevant procedure records.

## Implantation checklist

### Before the procedure

- [ ] Understand the rhythm diagnosis and device recommendation.
- [ ] Bring ECGs, monitoring reports and previous device information.
- [ ] Report fever, skin infection or new symptoms.
- [ ] Obtain explicit anticoagulant and diabetes-medicine instructions.
- [ ] Confirm fasting, admission time and caregiver arrangements.
- [ ] Ask how the device and follow-up are funded.

### On admission

- [ ] Carry identification, prescriptions, allergies and insurance documents.
- [ ] Tell staff about the last doses of relevant medicines.
- [ ] Explain problems with lying flat or previous anaesthesia.
- [ ] Confirm the planned implant site and the consent discussion.

### Before going home

- [ ] Obtain the device identification card and clinic contact number.
- [ ] Understand wound care and permitted arm or leg activity.
- [ ] Confirm the first device check and remote-monitoring instructions.
- [ ] Ask whom to contact if previous symptoms return.

## The first weeks and continuing device care

The team checks the device and assesses the wound before discharge. Some patients stay overnight; others follow a different pathway. Ask for a personal plan for bathing, exercise, driving and work. Returning symptoms should be assessed rather than dismissed as normal adjustment.

Seek urgent help for wound redness or swelling that increases, discharge, fever or worsening pain. Severe breathlessness, chest pain, collapse or major bleeding needs emergency assessment. A pacemaker does not make these symptoms safe to ignore.

Keep the device card accessible and tell healthcare professionals about the implant before scans, surgery or other procedures. MRI access depends on the complete system and a suitable checking protocol; do not assume either that all MRI is forbidden or that every scan is automatically safe. Attend scheduled checks even if you feel well.

## Mistakes that complicate preparation

1. **Calling every heart device a pacemaker.** Confirm what is actually being implanted.
2. **Changing blood thinners yourself.** Get a coordinated prescription plan.
3. **Hiding a fever to avoid postponement.** Infection matters for an implanted system.
4. **Planning travel without a device-clinic handover.** Know where checks will occur.
5. **Missing follow-up because symptoms improved.** Device function still needs assessment.

## Questions for the electrophysiologist

1. What documented rhythm makes pacing necessary?
2. How urgent is implantation?
3. Why is this device type appropriate?
4. What anaesthesia or sedation is planned?
5. How will my anticoagulant be managed?
6. What movement and work restrictions apply to me?
7. Where will device checks take place?
8. What should I do if I faint again?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a pacemaker treat all irregular heartbeats?

No. Different rhythm problems need different treatments. Ask what the device is intended to correct in your case.

### Will I be asleep for implantation?

Many implants use local anaesthesia, sometimes with sedation. Your team will explain the approach suitable for you.

### Should I stop my anticoagulant beforehand?

Only follow the implanting team's instructions. The correct plan depends on the drug, bleeding risk and reason for anticoagulation.

### Is a leadless pacemaker suitable for everyone?

No. The rhythm problem and pacing requirements determine suitability. It is an option for selected patients, not a universal upgrade.

### Can I move my shoulder afterwards?

Ask for a specific activity plan. Gentle movement and restrictions on heavier tasks need to be balanced according to your implant and wound.

### Can I have an MRI later?

Possibly, with assessment of the complete device system and the scan protocol. Always inform the imaging and device teams in advance.

### What should I carry when travelling?

Carry your device card, current medicines and clinic contact details. Discuss longer travel and follow-up timing before leaving the area.

### What if the original symptoms come back?

Contact the device clinic promptly; severe symptoms need emergency care. Do not assume a normal-looking wound proves that everything is working correctly.

Ask how the clinic monitors battery status and how you would be contacted if a generator replacement becomes necessary. This is a planned follow-up responsibility; do not wait for old symptoms to return before attending a scheduled check.

## Leave with a follow-up pathway

The most useful pacemaker surgery checklist continues beyond implantation. Understand the device, follow your personalised medicine plan and know how to access wound and device checks. A named clinic, identification card and clear symptom instructions are essential parts of discharge preparation.
